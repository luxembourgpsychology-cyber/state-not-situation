# French translation method for State. Not Situation.

Date: 2026-09-05
Written by: the lead French translator, after reading the almost-final text in full (pages 1 to 278) and before touching content/fr.ts.
Applies to: the whole book. First applied to website/content/fr.ts, which is the pilot for this method.
Amended: 2026-09-05, after the author issued translation/STANDARD.md. The standard governs. Where this method and the standard disagree, the standard wins; the amendments below bring the method into line with it, and a translation memory in the standard's format follows the glossary.
Amended again: 2026-09-06, after two independent reviews of the finished content/fr.ts. Where a review showed the method itself was short a rule, the rule is written in below and marked (2026-09-06); the scores and the tell recount for the website are at the end.

This is the method a second translator follows without me. It says what the book sounds like, what its words mean, what may move and what may not, and how to check a finished passage against yourself before anyone else sees it. Nothing here is a style preference. Every rule comes from a page of the book.

The author's instruction, in her words: "I want the context translated, not word by word." Translate what a sentence does to the reader, then write the French sentence a French writer would write to do that. Never add an idea, an image, a claim or a qualification the English does not have. Never drop a hedge, a limit or a distinction it does have. Plain English becomes plain French. Not warmer, not punchier, not more literary, not marketing.


## The book's voice in French

### What the English does

The book is written in ordinary words doing technical work. Its terms are story, reading, settings, loop, file, tab, brake, gap, draft, weather. None of them is a coined word and none is Latinate. The author chose kitchen vocabulary for neuroscience on purpose, and the French must do the same: histoire, lecture, réglages, boucle, dossier, onglet, frein, écart, brouillon, météo. A translator who reaches for "narratif", "paramètres", "processus itératif" or "réévaluation" has changed the book.

Sentences are declarative. The copula is everywhere and it is meant: "It is fast, it is old, and it is biased toward false alarms." "These instruments exist. You have them." The book almost never says "serves as", "constitutes" or "represents". In French, keep "est" and "a". Do not upgrade them to "constitue", "représente", "fait office de", "s'avère".

Sentence length varies a great deal, and the variance is the style. A forty-word sentence with three commas sits next to a four-word one: "The airspeed built." "He trusted his body." "Then stop." "Let it." Verbless fragments are used as full sentences: "Jaw. Breath. Shoulders." "Coffee first, before food." "Same morning. Same paragraph. Same Katrin. Different instrument settings." "State. Not situation." Repetition is deliberate and rhythmic: "His body was telling him something different. His body was telling him he was flying straight and level. His body felt right. His instruments felt wrong." The book repeats a word rather than rotating synonyms. So must the French.

The book almost never uses connectors. It juxtaposes. "The engine was running. The instruments were working. The data was right there." French translators add "en effet", "ainsi", "donc", "or", "dès lors", "par ailleurs" by reflex. Do not. Where the English has "so", "but", "and", "because", use "alors", "mais", "et", "parce que", and nothing more.

The humour is dry, exact and placed in asides: "an overture to an opera about chocolate", "a ratio of approximately fifty-seven to one", "asking her to un-sneeze", "the emotional sonar of a submarine", "litigating breakfast with a procedural limb", "which is thirteen for I heard you and have decided against", "Cooked. A term from no scientific literature and about as accurate as the literature manages." The jokes come from precision, not from exaggeration. In French, keep them where they are, keep them the same size, and do not explain them. Understatement translates into French without help.

The book refuses to overclaim, and the refusals are part of the argument. "The model knows its limits." "This book is not going to pretend she can." "It is only one study, in a young part of a young field." "Nothing in those pages is a metaphor until the book says it is one." "The move from a cockpit to a kitchen is mine." "Do not use this for gaslighting yourself." "Not every late night is a state error. Some are situations." Every one of these survives in French at the same strength. If a French sentence sounds more certain than its English, it is wrong.

### Three registers

The book moves between three registers, sometimes within a page, and the shifts are audible.

Narrative. Katrin, Marc, Léo, Mila, Antoine, David, Sophie. Present tense, timestamped sub-headings (06:52, 07:25, 15:28, 22:47), concrete Luxembourg places (Kirchberg, the Grund, Bonnevoie, the Pétrusse, Remich, Schengen, Cactus in Belair). Sentences are observational and unhurried, with exact small numbers (thirty-four minutes, one euro forty, a hundred and fifteen afternoons, twenty-three marks on a Post-it). The historical cases (the pilot, the Vincennes, Libby Zion, Utzon, Three Mile Island, Petrov) are told in the past tense, plainly, from the record.

Mechanism. "What's Actually Happening", the science. Named researchers, years, sample sizes, percentages, and always a limit attached: "A strong result on a small sample under an extreme condition." "Correlational, and the chapter treats it as a pattern rather than a mechanism." The vocabulary here is the vocabulary of the general science press (amygdale, cortex préfrontal, cortisol, noradrénaline, réseau du mode par défaut, système vestibulaire), never of a textbook.

Instruction. "The Pause", "The Experiment", "The Fix", "Try it." Second person, imperative, short: "Read it once. Stop. Wait twenty minutes. Do not reread." Every tool carries a stop rule and a "When It Is Not State" section that says when not to use it. Those are not disclaimers to be trimmed. They are half the tool.

### How chapters open and close

Chapters open in the middle of something, with a time or a date: "Your phone lights up at 22:47." "At 15:28, two minutes before it happens, Katrin's body already knows." "It is 22:30. You feel oddly capable." "On the morning of 3 July 1988 a guided-missile cruiser…" The first paragraph never announces what the chapter will do.

Chapters close on a short scene, a single line, a question or a fragment, and never on a summary: "Your body kept the keys. Let it." "Next Saturday she will walk along the Pétrusse. Not as a plan, more as an experiment." "Why was the threshold so low?" "The alarm is set for 06:40." "Read the dashboard. Delay the story. Take the reading again." Do not add a moral. Do not add a sentence that wraps up. If the English ends on a fragment, the French ends on a fragment.

Sub-headings inside chapters are short noun phrases in the English (The Break, The Loop, The Pause, The Itch After the Scratch, The Forbidden Zone, Speed Is Not Depth, The Second Engine, The Tab, The Fix). In French they take sentence case: La pause, La boucle, La démangeaison après la gratte, La zone interdite, La vitesse n'est pas la profondeur, Le second moteur, L'onglet, La réparation.

### Tense in French

Katrin scenes: présent, as in the English. "Katrin se réveille à 06:38, deux minutes avant le réveil."

Historical cases told in the English past: passé simple for the punctual events and imparfait for states. "Un petit avion monomoteur décolla du New Jersey. Il faisait route vers Martha's Vineyard." Passé simple is the conventional written French for this kind of narration and is not a tell. Keep to common third-person forms (décolla, s'engagea, monta, percuta, releva, lut, fit). If a form looks bookish (vécurent, résolurent), recast the sentence.

Dialogue and the reader's own past ("You have had this day. Everyone has had this day."): passé composé. "Vous avez eu cette journée. Tout le monde a eu cette journée."

General truths about the body: présent.

### The author's "I"

The author appears rarely and only as "I" (the Scientific Heartbeat, "The day I mean is the other kind", "You know the person I mean"). In French: "je", plainly. Never "nous" for the author, never "l'autrice de ces lignes".


## The glossary

Each entry gives the term, the French choice, the alternatives rejected and why, and where consistency must hold. Consistency means: the same French word every time the English uses the term as a term, across all sixteen chapters, the front matter, the case panels, the reference section and the website.

state. État. The spine of the book, always paired with situation: "State. Not situation." becomes "L'état. Pas la situation." and "Is this the situation? Or is this the state?" becomes "Est-ce la situation ? Ou est-ce l'état ?" Rejected: "condition" (medical), "disposition" (temperament), "état d'esprit" (adds the mind; the book's state is bodily first). Consistency: everywhere, including "state check" (contrôle de l'état), "state error" (erreur d'état), "the state was taught by the situation" (l'état a été appris par la situation).

situation. Situation. The same word. The French "situation" also means a job position; the context always disambiguates and the pair état/situation is too important to replace. Rejected: "circonstances" (plural, vague), "contexte" (the book uses context as a separate idea).

reading (noun), to read (verb). Lecture; lire. The book plays on both senses of the English word, reading a text and reading an instrument: "The first reading is informational. The second reading is interpretive." French "lecture" carries both senses, which is why it is the term and not "relevé". "What you found is a reading." becomes "Ce que vous avez trouvé, c'est une lecture." "take the reading" becomes "faire la lecture" or, when the act of measuring is meant, "relever" (verb). "prendre une lecture" is an anglicism and is banned. Rejected as the main term: "relevé" (loses the text sense and the pun on reading a message twice), "interprétation" (the book opposes reading to interpretation: a reading is a measurement taken before the story), "mesure" (the book uses "measure" and "measuring" separately; keep "mesurer" for those). Exception: where "reading" means a printed value on an instrument, as in the cover description "small instrument readings", use "relevés d'instruments"; that is the only place "relevé" appears as a noun. Consistency: "première lecture", "seconde lecture", "mode lecture" (reading mode), "lecture au vert" or "au propre" is not used; see clean panel. "misread", "misreading" become "erreur de lecture" (noun) and "lire de travers" or "mal lire" (verb). "The First Misreading" (page 10) becomes "La première erreur de lecture".

instrument, instrument panel, panel, dashboard, cockpit. Instrument; tableau de bord; tableau de bord (or "le tableau" once the panel has been named in the paragraph); tableau de bord; cockpit. The book uses panel, dashboard and instrument panel for the same thing; French collapses them into "tableau de bord" and loses nothing. Rejected: "poste de pilotage" for cockpit (formal; "cockpit" is the French word people use), "console", "cadran" for panel ("cadran" is one dial: "the dials on the panel" becomes "les cadrans du tableau de bord"). Aviation words: artificial horizon = horizon artificiel; altimeter = altimètre; airspeed indicator = indicateur de vitesse (not "badin", not "anémomètre", both jargon); level flight = vol en palier; to bank = s'incliner; graveyard spiral = spirale de la mort (the French term; "spirale du cimetière" is a calque nobody uses); inner ear = oreille interne; vestibular system = système vestibulaire; "the nose dropped" = "le nez tomba" (2026-09-06: not "le nez piqua". French flight instruction says "le nez tombe"; "piquer" carries the nose-down attitude only inside "piquer du nez", and standing alone a general reader hears the transitive verb, to sting. The English "The nose dropped." is flat and the French must be flat too).

clean panel, green across the panel, warm panel. Tableau de bord au vert; tout le tableau au vert; tableau de bord qui chauffe. The book's "clean" means no upstream cause found, and the chapter itself says "All signals are green. Green across the panel." "Le tableau de bord au vert" is the French a dashboard reader says; "The Clean Panel" (Chapter Twelve) becomes "Le tableau de bord au vert" and the Chapter Thirteen panel line "The check came back green." becomes "Le contrôle est revenu au vert." Rejected: "propre" (reads as hygiene), "vierge" (means blank), "net" (means sharp). "warm panel" and "running warm": "un tableau de bord qui chauffe", "son système chauffe". Rejected: "chaud" as an adjective on its own ("tableau chaud" says nothing in French).

settings, instrument settings. Réglages; réglages de l'instrument. "Same life. Different instrument settings." becomes "Même vie. Instruments réglés autrement." (the verb form is the French a cover would carry; the literal "Réglages d'instruments différents" is not a sentence a French writer would print). "the settings were off" becomes "les réglages étaient faussés". "You cannot know until you have seen the settings." becomes "Vous ne pouvez pas le savoir tant que vous n'avez pas vu les réglages." Rejected: "paramètres" (software), "calibrage" and "étalonnage" (reserved for the book's separate word "calibration": "calibrated" = "calibré", "miscalibrated" = "mal calibré", "calibration" = "calibrage").

signal. Signal. "The body speaks first" is "signal and story": "le signal et l'histoire". "Your body produces signals" = "votre corps produit des signaux". No alternative. Consistency: never use "signal" for cue (next entry).

cue. Déclencheur. The Pavlovian sense: the thing a trained sequence fires on (the clock at 15:28, the coffee machine's cycle, the phone within reach, the moment Antoine registers disagreement). "The Predictable Cue" becomes "Le déclencheur prévisible"; "change the cue" becomes "changer le déclencheur"; "the next cue" in the Attention definition becomes "le prochain déclencheur". Rejected: "signal" (collides with signal, which the book keeps apart: "The threat response responds to signal and not to context." against "Cues train predictions."), "stimulus" (laboratory register), "indice" (cognitive-psychology jargon, and in the book's investigation frame "indice" reads as a clue), "amorce" (too technical). Consistency: everywhere the English says cue.

story, narrating mode, narrator, scenario. Histoire; mode récit; le narrateur; scénario. "Before you believe the story." becomes "Avant de croire l'histoire." "the story feels like the truth" becomes "l'histoire a l'air vraie". "reading mode" against "narrating mode" becomes "mode lecture" against "mode récit". Rejected: "récit" for story (literary; keep "récit" only inside "mode récit"), "narration" (abstract), "version" (the book uses "the first draft" for that). "scenario" is the book's own separate word for the rehearsed futures Katrin builds: "scénario".

first reading, second reading. Première lecture; seconde lecture. "seconde" because there are exactly two; "deuxième" is accepted where the sentence needs it. "Your first reading is not the whole story." becomes "Votre première lecture n'est pas toute l'histoire." Rejected: "le fin mot de l'histoire" (an idiom that means the real explanation, which is not what the strap says; the strap says the first reading is incomplete).

first draft, draft. Premier jet; brouillon. "the first draft launched the missiles" becomes "le premier jet a lancé les missiles"; "the body's confident weather report about the world is only a draft" becomes "n'est qu'un brouillon". Rejected: "ébauche" (a sketch), "version préliminaire" (a report). The state-line legend "first drafts, misreads" becomes "premiers jets, erreurs de lecture".

the three loops: Time, Attention, Safety. Les trois boucles : Temps, Attention, Sécurité. Capitalised as names when used as names: "la boucle Temps", "la boucle Sécurité", "le loop Attention" is never written. "Safety" is Sécurité because the system asks "safe or not safe" ("en sécurité ou pas"). Rejected: "Sûreté" (legal and industrial), "Menace" (changes the sense: the system reads safety, its bias is toward threat), "Danger". "loop" is "boucle" everywhere: checking loop = boucle de vérification, habit loop = boucle d'habitude, the loops stacked = les boucles se sont empilées, stacked loops = boucles empilées.

system (the three systems). Système. "Three systems ran through Katrin's morning." becomes "Trois systèmes ont tourné pendant toute la matinée de Katrin." (Amended under the standard. "ont traversé la matinée" was grammatical but carried the English image; "tourner" is what a system does in French, and it matches the sentences that follow: "Le Temps a démarré à 06:38 et ne s'est jamais arrêté.") The book says the three are "a sorting tool", "un outil de tri". (2026-09-06: "three systems that shape how your body's signals get weighted" is "trois systèmes qui règlent le poids que prennent les signaux de votre corps". "Influer sur" is weaker than "shape" and makes the three one factor among others, where page 13 presents them as what sets the weighting; "déterminer" overcorrects. "Régler" sits at the English strength and is the book's own verb, the one under réglages.)

case, case evidence, the investigation, verified, input. Cas; pièces du dossier; l'enquête; vérifié; entrée. "16 cases. Three readings." becomes "16 cas. Trois lectures." "CASE EVIDENCE / 00" becomes "PIÈCES DU DOSSIER / 00". "VERIFIED EVENT", "VERIFIED TONE", "VERIFIED CRISIS", "VERIFIED THREAT", "VERIFIED MONDAY", "NEXT READING" become "Événement vérifié", "Ton vérifié", "Crise vérifiée", "Menace vérifiée", "Lundi vérifié", "Lecture suivante" (adjective after the noun, as French requires). "INPUT" as a panel label is "Entrée". In prose, "input" is never "entrée"; it becomes "ce qui arrive", "ce qui entre", "la sollicitation", "le message", whichever the sentence means. (2026-09-06: nor is it a nominalised adjective. "Le neutre" reads in a table of contents as the grammatical neuter or the electrical neutral, and it is an abstraction in a list that is concrete throughout. Chapter Two is "Pourquoi le manque de sommeil fait paraître hostile ce qui est neutre": the cause keeps the subject position the English gives it, "paraître" keeps the thing from being asserted to be hostile, and "ce qui est neutre" is a thing, not a category. A chapter title that turns a stated cause into a trailing "quand…" clause has changed a causal claim into a temporal one, which the back check forbids; "rend hostile" strengthens it the other way and is refused too.) Rejected: "pièces à conviction" (says a crime), "preuves" for evidence in this frame (the book's evidence is a case file, not a proof), "éléments" (bureaucratic). Note that "dossier" carries a second sense in the book, next entry, and the two senses do not collide in practice.

open file, tab, to file, classification. Dossier ouvert; onglet; classer; classement. "The Open File" becomes "Le dossier ouvert"; "the tab is open" becomes "l'onglet est ouvert"; "the brain cannot file probably" becomes "le cerveau ne peut pas classer probablement"; "a table number" (the waiter's) stays "un numéro de table". Rejected: "fichier" (a computer file, and the book's file is a paper one that goes in a drawer), "affaire" (legal).

supply chain, upstream, the first domino. Chaîne d'approvisionnement; en amont; le premier domino. The book's own industrial image, kept: "run the supply chain" = "remonter la chaîne d'approvisionnement", "the upstream point" = "le point en amont". Rejected: "les causes" (loses the image the book uses in six chapters).

the gap. L'écart. "Between the body's first reaction and your response, there is a gap." becomes "Entre la première réaction du corps et votre réponse, il y a un écart." Also for the two-second silence the hand fills: "the gap stays empty" = "l'écart reste vide". Rejected: "fossé" (too wide), "faille" (a fault), "intervalle" (only time; the book's gap is both time and space), "brèche" (military).

professional support. L'aide d'un professionnel. (2026-09-06.) Page 14, "This book does not replace professional support", becomes "Ce livre ne remplace pas l'aide d'un professionnel". Rejected: "accompagnement professionnel", which in French most often names career coaching and professional development, so the one sentence on the page where a clinical psychologist states the limit of her book would be read as a limit about careers. Also rejected: "un suivi professionnel" (narrows the broad English "support" to ongoing therapy), "une prise en charge" (administrative).

settled, established. Consolidé; établi. (2026-09-06.) Page 13 runs three tiers in three sentences and the French must keep three words: "decades of replicated research" = "des décennies de recherches répliquées"; "promising but not yet settled" = "prometteurs mais pas encore consolidés"; "plausible extensions of established science" = "des prolongements plausibles d'une science établie". "Settled truth", in the next paragraph, stays "une vérité établie". Using "établi" for both "settled" and "established" one sentence apart flattens the distinction the passage exists to draw.

evidence, finding. Preuve ; étayer ; résultat. (2026-09-06.) The noun holds as the term, and above all in the grades, "niveau de preuve élevé, moyen, faible", which is the French frame. But French does not hang a quantity or a replication on it: it étaye une affirmation and it réplique un résultat. Page 13, "those claims carry different amounts of evidence" is "ces affirmations ne sont pas toutes également étayées", and "when a finding rests on solid, replicated evidence" is "quand un résultat est solidement étayé et répliqué": the replication moves onto the résultat, which is what French replicates, and no claim, hedge or tier moves with it. Rejected: "la même quantité de preuves" and "des preuves solides et répliquées", both grammatical, both English collocations carried across, and both the one place a native reader heard English under the French. "Des décennies de recherches répliquées" stands: French does replicate recherches. Page 226, where "evidence" names the empirical material another paper explains differently, the French is "les mêmes résultats"; "expliquer les mêmes preuves" is not French, and "les mêmes données" would take the word the book keeps for the panel. That one is a term swap and not a collocation repair, so it is open in QUERIES-fr.md; use "les mêmes résultats" until the author rules.

the brake, the pause, the itch, phantom load, the treadmill, the counterfeit, soft fascination, the narrator, the stack. Le frein; la pause; la démangeaison; la charge fantôme; le tapis roulant; la contrefaçon; la fascination douce; le narrateur; l'empilement (verb: s'empiler). All are the book's images and all cross into French as they are. "The Relief That Becomes Itch" becomes "Le soulagement qui tourne à la démangeaison".

smoke detector, toast, fire, false alarm. Détecteur de fumée; pain grillé; feu; fausse alerte. "Most of the time it is toast." becomes "La plupart du temps, c'est du pain grillé." "It rings for toast far more often than for fire." becomes "Il sonne bien plus souvent pour du pain grillé que pour un feu." Rejected: "toast" untranslated (French "toast" is the glass you raise).

weather, weather report, forecast, climate. Météo; bulletin météo; prévision; climat. "the body's confident weather report about the world" = "le bulletin météo plein d'assurance que le corps établit sur le monde". "The Forecast" (Chapter Fifteen) = "La prévision". "climate rather than weather" = "le climat plutôt que la météo". "the household has a weather pattern" = "cette maison a une météo". Rejected: "temps" for weather (collides with Temps, the loop).

rest, restore, restoration, recover, recovery. Repos, se reposer; ressourcer; ressourcement; récupérer; récupération. The book distinguishes rested from restored: "reposé" against "ressourcé". "Why Rest Doesn't Always Restore" becomes "Pourquoi le repos ne ressource pas toujours". Rejected: "restaurer" (a building, or a meal), "réparer" (says something was broken), "régénérer" (cosmetics).

threshold, baseline. Seuil; niveau de base. "the threshold drops" = "le seuil baisse"; "back to baseline" = "revenu au niveau de base"; "baseline sensitivity" = "la sensibilité de base"; "baseline sensitivity rises" = "la sensibilité de base augmente" (amended under the standard: never "monte", which mirrors English conceptual syntax). Rejected: "ligne de base" (a calque), "référence", "anxiété" for baseline sensitivity (a different concept).

confidence marker, high, medium, low evidence, the Scientific Heartbeat. Marqueur de confiance; niveau de preuve élevé, moyen, faible; Le pouls scientifique. The markers are heartbeat traces in the margin and the reference section is named after them. "pouls" is the French for a heartbeat trace and reads as a title; the grade descriptions then follow it: "Pouls net et fort." "Irrégulier, amplitude plus faible." "Presque plat, légère ondulation." Rejected: "battement" (correct for one beat, odd as a section name), "cœur", "électrocardiogramme". In the reference section, "BUILT ON" and "PUSHED AGAINST" are proposed as "S'appuie sur" and "Se heurte à"; this is a team decision to confirm before the reference section is translated, and it is not on the website.

the body speaks first, the mind explains second. Le corps parle d'abord. L'esprit explique ensuite. Fixed. Within a sentence: "Votre corps parle d'abord et votre esprit explique ensuite."

same life, different instrument settings. Même vie. Instruments réglés autrement. Fixed, see settings.

Katrin's readings. Jaw, breath (breathing), shoulders, energy, thoughts = mâchoire, respiration, épaules, énergie, pensées. "Check your jaw." = "Observez votre mâchoire." "Check your breath." = "Observez votre respiration." "Check your shoulders." = "Observez vos épaules." (Amended under the standard: "vérifier sa mâchoire" is checking whether something is wrong with it; the exercise is interoceptive noticing, so the verb is "observer", or "portez votre attention sur" where the sentence has room. "Vérifiez" is withdrawn for every reading instruction in the book.) "Jaw. Breath. Shoulders." = "Mâchoire. Respiration. Épaules." Rejected: "souffle" (poetic, and "à bout de souffle" leaks in). "tight" jaw = "serrée"; "loose" = "détendue"; breathing "high in the chest or low near the ribs" = "haute dans la poitrine ou basse près des côtes"; shoulders "lifted or dropped" = "levées ou relâchées"; energy "flat, wired, or steady" = "à plat, à cran, ou stable".

the reader. Le lecteur, masculine generic. Never "le lecteur ou la lectrice", never "lecteur·rice", never "les lectrices et lecteurs".

the author. L'autrice. The form the French-language press in Luxembourg and most French publishers now use. Alternatives "auteure" and "auteur" are acceptable French; the choice should be confirmed with the author once and then held everywhere.

## Queries

(Rewritten 2026-09-06, under the TRANSLATION QUERY FORMAT addendum the author added to the standard the same day.)

A query is not a way of handing the author a decision. Where the analysis makes one option clearly superior, take it, put it in the file, and write the reason here as a memory row or a rule. Write a query only where the choice could materially change meaning, metaphor, scientific precision, characterisation or the conceptual architecture of the book: a term that will recur across a section not yet translated, a claim that would be strengthened, an image the book builds elsewhere, a distinction the argument stands on. A phrase that is merely hard, and a choice between two wordings that say the same thing at the same strength, are the translator's to settle.

Every query that survives that test goes in translation/QUERIES-fr.md, complete, with nothing compressed:

SOURCE, the English sentence, with one sentence before and after where context matters. CURRENT TRANSLATION, quoted from the file as it stands. ISSUE, one or two sentences, naming the category: semantic fidelity, scientific terminology, idiom, metaphor, register, collocation, cultural localisation, conceptual consistency. OPTION A, the most semantically faithful native formulation, written out as a whole sentence. OPTION B, the most stylistically natural, written out as a whole sentence. OPTION C only where the choice is genuinely conceptual and not lexical. RECOMMENDATION, one option and the tradeoff in one sentence. CONFIDENCE, High, Medium or Low.

Use the recommendation in the file and move on; the file always carries a finished sentence, never a blank, a bracket or an alternative. Where the English itself is unclear, write SOURCE QUERY in the same format, with the options written as choices about the English or about the site rather than about the French. The author reads that file.


## How to handle the reader address

The book addresses the reader as "you" from its first page to its last, and it means one person. In French that is "vous", always. Never "tu". Never "on" as a substitute for "you" ("on ne remarque pas que l'horizon a disparu" is wrong; "vous ne remarquez pas que l'horizon a disparu" is right). "on" is allowed only where the English itself is impersonal ("people", "one", "everyone does").

Imperatives stay imperatives, in the vous form, and stay as short as the English: "Check your jaw." = "Observez votre mâchoire." "Stop." = "Arrêtez-vous." "Try it." = "Essayez." "Then stop." = "Puis arrêtez." "Do not reread." = "Ne relisez pas." "Put the tool down." = "Posez l'outil." Do not soften them with "n'hésitez pas à", "vous pouvez", "essayez de", "il est conseillé de". Those are additions.

Questions to the reader keep their shape and number. "Is this the situation? Or is this the state?" is two questions and stays two. "Where did this state come from? Sleep, food, caffeine, an unresolved conversation, the time of day?" keeps its list inside the question.

"we" and "our", when the author includes herself with the reader, are "nous" and "notre" ("when we learn where our first reading came from" = "quand nous apprenons d'où venait notre première lecture"). "on" is possible there but "nous" keeps the register of a written book.

"your career", "your relationship", "your manager": "votre carrière", "votre relation", "votre manager" ("manager" is the word people use in Luxembourg offices; "responsable" is acceptable; "supérieur hiérarchique" is not this book).

Pronoun gender. The reader is not gendered in English and is not gendered in French. Avoid past participles that would force an agreement on "vous" ("vous êtes reposé" gives the reader a gender). Recast: "vous avez dormi", "vous avez récupéré", "le sommeil a fait son travail". Where a participle is unavoidable, use the masculine generic, once, without a bracketed e.

The book's "I", when it appears, is "je". "The day I mean" = "La journée dont je parle". "You know the person I mean." = "Vous voyez de qui je parle."


## Sentence rhythm rules

1. Measure before you translate. For any passage, note the shortest and longest sentence in the English and where the fragments are. The French must have a sentence as short as the shortest English one, and its longest must not exceed the longest English one by more than a third. The average does not matter; the spread does.

2. Short stays short. A sentence of two to five English words becomes two to six French words. "He trusted his body." = "Il fit confiance à son corps." "It stops moving." = "Il ne bouge plus." "You have them." = "Vous les avez." If French grammar makes it longer (negation, articles, agreement), cut a word somewhere else in the same paragraph.

3. Fragments stay fragments. "Jaw. Breath. Shoulders." = "Mâchoire. Respiration. Épaules." "Not for long, stop for five seconds." = "Pas longtemps, cinq secondes." "The tiredness that presents itself as a question about your career." stays a verbless sentence: "La fatigue qui se présente comme une question sur votre carrière." Do not attach a fragment to the previous sentence with a colon or a relative pronoun.

4. Do not merge. French translators join two short English sentences into one with "qui", "ce qui", "et", "dont". Every merge flattens the book. Two sentences in English are two sentences in French unless the French would be ungrammatical.

5. Split only under pressure. A long accumulative English sentence may be split into two French sentences only when the French would otherwise need more than two subordinate clauses or an unreadable chain of "que". When you split, keep the order of ideas.

6. Keep the anaphora. "His body was telling him… His body was telling him… His body felt right. His instruments felt wrong." = "Son corps lui disait… Son corps lui disait… Son corps sonnait juste. Ses instruments sonnaient faux." Repetition that is in the English is in the French. Do not replace the second occurrence with a pronoun or a synonym.

7. Keep the lists bare. "Heart rate, jaw tension, breathing depth, shoulder position, the speed of your thoughts." has no "and" and no verb. The French has no "et" and no verb.

8. Short after long. The book follows a long sentence with a short one that lands. Preserve the landing. If the French long sentence has grown, the short one must not.

9. Interruptions stay where they are. "The explanation, because it arrives with the full weight of physical conviction, the tight jaw, the fast heartbeat, the heat behind the ears, feels like deep knowledge." keeps its inserted "parce que" clause in the middle: "L'explication, parce qu'elle arrive avec tout le poids de la conviction physique, la mâchoire serrée, le cœur qui bat vite, la chaleur derrière les oreilles, donne l'impression d'un savoir profond." Moving the clause to the front tidies what the author left untidy.

10. Paragraph endings. The book ends paragraphs on a plain sentence that does not sum up ("She will connect it to the day." "This is safety." "You have the time."). Never add a closing sentence. Never turn the last sentence into a maxim.

10b. Verbs of speech are counted. (2026-09-06.) The book gives the story a voice and withholds one from the data: "It said I am failing; the data was I am tired." The conclusion speaks; the data only is. A French "les données disaient" hands the data a voice the English refuses it, and flattens the signal-and-story asymmetry the line exists to carry. The French is "Ça disait je suis en train d'échouer ; les données, c'était je suis fatiguée.": the dislocation carries "was" in the same colloquial register as "Ça disait", and takes no colon, because the English takes none.

11. Wordy human constructions survive. "the fact that", "as a result of", "the sort of", "something closer to", "which is to say", "in the sense that": "le fait que", "du fait de", "le genre de", "quelque chose qui ressemble plutôt à", "c'est-à-dire", "au sens où". Do not tighten them.

12. Hedges and intensifiers survive at their size. "about 300 hours" = "environ 300 heures"; "roughly twenty minutes" = "à peu près vingt minutes"; "a bit short" = "un peu court"; "very slightly higher" = "très légèrement plus haute"; "probably" = "probablement"; "perhaps once a week" = "peut-être une fois par semaine"; "tends to" = "a tendance à"; "in all likelihood" = "selon toute vraisemblance". Never promote "may" to "will", "often" to "always", "some" to "many".


## The idiom policy

Sense over image. An English idiom is replaced by the French idiom that does the same work, not by a word-for-word picture. "killed on impact" = "tués sur le coup". "is one sentence long" = "tient en une phrase". "a literal instruction" = "une consigne à prendre au pied de la lettre". "which is which" = "lesquelles sont lesquelles". "survives a closer look" = "tient, à y regarder de plus près". "case closed" = "affaire classée". "the morning is the bill for the evening" = "le matin est la facture de la soirée". "charging rent" = "se met à réclamer un loyer" (2026-09-06: corrected here to agree with the metaphor policy below and with the file. "Faire payer un loyer" is the English verb carried across; "réclamer un loyer" is what a landlord does in French, and the joke stays dry). "on the shelf" = "sur l'étagère" (the generous reading "is the first one to fall off the shelf" = "est la première à tomber de l'étagère", the image crosses). "the room is bracing" = "la pièce se prépare au choc" or "la pièce se raidit". "cooked" (by Sunday we're both cooked) = "cuits" (the French say exactly this). "none of it had to happen the way it did" = "rien de tout cela n'avait à se passer ainsi" (2026-09-06: "avoir à" is the plain French modal and takes an inanimate subject without complaint. "Être obligé de" on an abstract subject is heard in speech but loose in set prose, and "obligé" imports an agent doing the obliging that the plain English "had to" does not have). "this explanation felt like the event" = "cette explication est passée pour l'événement" (2026-09-06: the mechanism is that the explanation is taken for the event. "Avait tout de l'événement" states an objective resemblance the narrator vouches for, a shade stronger than the English report of a feeling, and its imparfait sits badly beside "a parlé" and "a expliqué").

The book's own images are terms, not idioms, and they cross unchanged: the cockpit, the instrument panel, the dashboard, the weather report, the supply chain, the phantom load, the smoke detector, toast and fire, the treadmill and the road, the open file, the tab, the table number, the load-bearing wall, the Skinner box in your pocket, the lever and the pellet, the hourglass and the clock, the graveyard spiral. Translate the words of the image; keep the image.

When the English word is a calque waiting to happen, find the French object. "graveyard spiral" is "spirale de la mort", the term French pilots use. "dead light" on the case panel is the dead fluorescent tube of the chapter, so "néon mort". "open screen" is a screen that is on, so "écran allumé". "low fuel" on a person is "réservoir bas". "a depleted day" is "une journée à plat". "the emotional sonar of a submarine" is "le sonar émotionnel d'un sous-marin" (the image is universal).

Units. Keep the figure the book gives. Where the English figure is itself an approximation for the reader's benefit, give the French reader the same benefit: "nearly a mile every sixty seconds" = "près d'un kilomètre et demi toutes les soixante secondes"; "six inches from his eyes" = "à quinze centimètres de ses yeux". Where the figure comes from a record and is quoted as such, keep the unit: "4 700 pieds par minute" stays in feet, because the accident brief is in feet. Temperatures are already Celsius. Money is already euros. On the press page the format line gives both: "15,2 × 22,9 cm (6 × 9 pouces)".

Science. Use the term the French general science press uses, not the textbook term and not the English: amygdale, cortex préfrontal, cortisol, noradrénaline, adrénaline, adénosine, mélatonine, réseau du mode par défaut, système vestibulaire, interoception, proprioception, cortex orbitofrontal, insula antérieure, striatum, ganglions de la base. Named phenomena take their established French name where one exists (le conditionnement pavlovien, le principe du détecteur de fumée, l'effet Zeigarnik, la théorie de la restauration de l'attention, la zone de maintien de l'éveil, la procrastination du coucher, l'orthosomnie, la théorie polyvagale) and otherwise a plain gloss. Never invent a French term to sound scientific.

Humour. Keep it dry and in the same place. Do not add a wink ("comme on dit", "si l'on peut dire", "pour ainsi dire"). Do not explain it. If a joke depends on an English-only fact, keep the sentence's function (a precise, slightly absurd detail) with a detail that is true in the scene.

Register. The book puts a technical word next to a kitchen word in one sentence and does not apologise. "Her amygdala heard the word and fired." "The bolognese is burning. Neither of them can smell it." Keep the mix. Do not raise the kitchen word to match the technical one.

What the policy forbids. Making it warmer (adding "doucement", "tendrement", "tout simplement"). Making it punchier (adding "vraiment", "littéralement", "purement et simplement", exclamation marks). Making it more literary (past-participle openings, inversions, "tel", "or", "point" for "pas"). Making it marketing (adding "essentiel", "précieux", "unique", "puissant"). Explaining a reference the book leaves unexplained. Correcting the author's argument.


## Metaphor policy (from the standard)

Classify every metaphor before translating it.

Type A, works naturally in both languages: preserve it. The cockpit, the instrument panel, the horizon, the smoke detector, toast and fire, the open file, the weather report, the forecast, the first draft, the supply chain, the brake, rainy weather behind glass.

Type B, exists in French but sounds less natural: adapt it. "Three systems ran through Katrin's morning" (systems "tournent" in French, they do not "traverse" a morning). "starts charging rent" ("se met à réclamer un loyer": the French verb a landlord takes). "the body's confident weather report" ("le bulletin météo plein d'assurance que le corps établit").

Type C, confusing or unintentionally comic in French: translate the mechanism. The English cognitive-cost vocabulary is the standing case. "the cheapest available input" is not "la sollicitation la moins coûteuse à disposition" and "the expensive work" is not "le travail qui coûte cher"; the mechanism is effort, so "ce qui demande le moins d'effort" and "le travail exigeant". Keep the monetary image only where the book builds it on purpose in a chapter ("the morning is the bill for the evening" stays "le matin est la facture de la soirée"; that image is the chapter's own and the French says it).

Type D, central to the book's identity: preserve, with a light adjustment if needed. State against situation; reading against story; the instrument and its settings; the body speaks first, the mind explains second; the reading as both a text read and a measurement read (kept on "lecture", which carries both senses).

Do not silently remove a recurring book metaphor. If one cannot cross, write a TRANSLATION QUERY (see Queries below).


## What must never change

Claims. Every claim in a sentence appears once in the French, at the same strength, with the same subject. "Sleep loss does not primarily make you more irritable. It makes you less able to access the emotional register for generosity, patience, benefit of the doubt." keeps "primarily" and keeps both halves.

Hedges, limits and qualifications. "Not by a lot, but just enough." "Suggestive but incomplete." "A plausible hypothesis that needs stronger tests." "This is a spectrum, not a switch." "The magnitude is different. The architecture is identical." "The direction transfers. The size does not." Every one, at size. A translator who drops "roughly", "about", "in all likelihood", "may", "sometimes", "for most people", "the chapter compresses that" has changed a claim.

The stop rules and the "When It Is Not State" sections. Complete, unsoftened, in the same position.

The confidence markers and their levels. High, medium, low as printed with each chapter.

The refusals. "The model knows its limits." "This book is not going to pretend she can." "The sentence stands as written and it says the opposite of its source." These are the book's honesty and they are quoted by reviewers.

The title. "State. Not Situation." is untranslated everywhere, including inside French sentences, with its two full stops, and takes no italics, no guillemets and no French article. "State. Not Situation. est son premier livre." No French edition exists, and the title is the author's.

The cover lines. "Time · Attention · Safety" = "Temps · Attention · Sécurité". ## Typography

Spaces. A narrow no-break space (U+202F) before ; ! ? and inside « and ». A no-break space (U+00A0) before :. Never a regular space before any of these, never nothing. In the website files these are literal characters in the string, not entities.

Quotation marks. « and » for every quotation, including the case panels ("ok." becomes « ok. »), the FAA sentence, and dialogue. English curly quotes never appear in French text. Quotation inside quotation uses “ and ”.

Apostrophe. The curly apostrophe ’ (U+2019) everywhere. Never the straight '.

Euphonic "l'on". After où, et, ou, si, que and qui, edited French prose sets "l'on" and not "on" (2026-09-06): "la page de titre, où l'on lit State. Not Situation." It matters most in alt text, which a screen reader speaks aloud and where the hiatus is audible.

Dashes. None. The book has none; it uses commas for asides. No em dash (U+2014), no en dash (U+2013) for asides. The hyphen stays for compounds (e-mail, Post-it, sous-marin, Kennedy Jr stays without a full stop, as printed).

Ellipsis. The single character … (U+2026) with no space before it, only where the English has one ("I am waiting for…").

Numbers. Thousands with a narrow no-break space: 4 700, 2 617. Decimals with a comma: 15,2. Times as printed: 06:38. Percentages: "soixante pour cent" when the English spells it, "60 %" with a narrow no-break space when the English uses the sign.

Capitals. French sentence case for every heading, chapter title and sub-heading: "Une journée qui aurait dû bien se passer", "Le radar avait raison", "Le dossier ouvert". No Title Case. Accents on capitals: É, À, Ê ("Événement vérifié", "À paraître", "Écouter"). The loop names are capitalised when they name the loop (Temps, Attention, Sécurité) and lowercase when they are ordinary words ("le temps qu'il atteigne la côte").

Italics and bold. The book italicises thoughts and reported inner speech in places ("I am waiting for…", "this is the part where you stand"). The website has no italics; those phrases are set in roman inside the sentence, as the English website file already does. Bold never appears in prose.

Words. "e-mail" with a hyphen (the word used in Luxembourg; "courriel" is not this book; "mail" alone is spoken). "week-end" with a hyphen. "Post-it" capitalised. "Wi-Fi" as the English prints it. "smartphone" and "tram" unchanged. "manager" unchanged. "Instagram", "LinkedIn", "Fitbit" unchanged.

The reader is "vous" and the ordinary present tense is used for the reader's present ("pendant que vous lisez cette phrase").
