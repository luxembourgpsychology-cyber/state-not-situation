TRANSLATION QUERY
Source: "It works in the gap between fine and clinical, the space where most people live on most days." (page 14, book.readersAnchor)
Issue: English can set two bare words, "fine" and "clinical", as the two ends of a scale. French cannot without marking them: "entre ça va et clinique" is unreadable, and "clinique" alone reads as the noun (a clinic). Guillemets mark them as labels; the alternative names the scale with a word the English does not have.
Option A: Il travaille dans l’écart entre « ça va » et « clinique », l’espace où la plupart des gens vivent la plupart des jours.
Option B: Il travaille dans l’écart entre « ça va » et le seuil clinique, l’espace où la plupart des gens vivent la plupart des jours.
Recommendation: Option A. It adds punctuation, not vocabulary, and keeps your two poles as you set them. Option B is smoother but "seuil" is a word of the book (the threshold that drops) and would put it where you did not.

TRANSLATION QUERY
Source: "An unanswered message starts charging rent." (page 10, misreading.readings[3])
Issue: The English turns the "rent-free" idiom inside out. French has the borrowed idiom ("vivre dans la tête de quelqu’un sans payer de loyer") but not its inversion, so a literal "commence à faire payer un loyer" reads as a calque. Type B metaphor under your policy: it exists, it needs adapting.
Option A: Un message sans réponse se met à réclamer un loyer.
Option B: Un message sans réponse commence à coûter.
Recommendation: Option A. "Réclamer un loyer" is the verb a landlord takes in French, so the image stays and stays dry. Option B keeps only the mechanism and loses the joke; the page 10 lines are the book’s wit in five rows, and this is the one that carries it.

TRANSLATION QUERY
Source: "It said I am failing; the data was I am tired." (page 20, cases.closing)
Issue: Two things at once. First, the pronoun: in the book "It" is the conclusion forming in Katrin at 08:05, and on the site the line stands alone under the case cards, so the French pronoun has no antecedent either. "Elle disait" would be read as "she said" (Katrin), which is a different sentence; "Ça disait" is unambiguous but spoken in register; "La conclusion disait" is clear but adds a noun you did not write. Second, and this is the one the file now turns on: you give the conclusion a verb of speech and you refuse the data one. It said. The data was. A French "les données disaient" hands the data a voice you withheld from it, and that asymmetry is the whole line. The dislocation "les données, c’était" is the only way I found to say "was" in the same register as "Ça disait".
Option A: Ça disait je suis en train d’échouer ; les données, c’était je suis fatiguée.
Option B: La conclusion disait je suis en train d’échouer ; les données, c’était je suis fatiguée.
Recommendation: Option A, which is what the file now carries. It adds nothing, the register drop is the size of your "It said", and the data keeps its silence. If you would rather the site carried the antecedent, Option B is the honest way to do it, and "conclusion" is the word page 20 uses two sentences earlier. Neither option takes a colon before "je suis fatiguée", because your English takes none; if you want the reading eased, a colon in both halves is the smallest change.

TRANSLATION QUERY
Source: "You have had this day. Everyone has had this day. You have had it dozens of times and you will have it again…" (page 12, knowTheDay.runs[1])
Issue: Your verb is the flattest one English has, and you repeat it four times. French has to choose. "Avoir cette journée" is equally flat and equally slightly odd, and it keeps the repetition visible. "Vivre cette journée" is the collocation a French writer reaches for first, but it is a shade more emphatic than "have had", and the standard forbids making a sentence punchier than its source. The method's own memory prints Option A, and the file had drifted to Option B without a note.
Option A: Vous avez eu cette journée. Tout le monde a eu cette journée. Vous l’avez eue des dizaines de fois et vous l’aurez encore…
Option B: Vous avez vécu cette journée. Tout le monde a vécu cette journée. Vous l’avez vécue des dizaines de fois et vous la vivrez encore…
Recommendation: Option A, restored, and it is what the file now carries. It holds the plain verb you wrote and the four repetitions, and the anaphora lands the same way in both. Option B reads a little more easily aloud; if you prefer it, say so and I will amend the method rather than leave the book and the website saying it differently.

TRANSLATION QUERY
Source: "…a list of the work the chapter is built on, and a list of the work that limits it, complicates it, or explains the same evidence differently." (page 226, map.heartbeat[0] and press.description[3])
Issue: The file holds evidence as preuve and finding as résultat, and here evidence is standing as résultat. French explains results, not proofs: "expliquer autrement les mêmes preuves" is not a sentence a French researcher writes, and "les mêmes données" would take the word the book keeps for the panel ("les données étaient là depuis le début"). The referent is the same either way and no reader is misled; what is at stake is the term, and it will recur on every page of the reference section.
Option A: …ou expliquent autrement les mêmes résultats.
Option B: …ou rendent autrement compte des mêmes preuves.
Recommendation: Option A, which is what the file carries, and which I would apply to the whole book. It is the sentence a French scientist writes about rival explanations of the same material, and the term preuve is not lost: it holds the grades on page 13, where the French frame is "niveau de preuve élevé, moyen, faible". Option B keeps the term in this one sentence, but "rendre compte de preuves" is the same imported collocation the page 13 block was just repaired for, and it buys consistency at the price of the naturalness the standard ranks above it. If you would rather the term were held everywhere, say so and I will take Option B here and reopen page 13 with you.
