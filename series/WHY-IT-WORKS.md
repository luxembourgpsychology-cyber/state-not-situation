# Why it works

A briefing for the author on why two of her videos travelled and two did not, written
10 November 2026 from searches run that day. Every factual sentence below carries its
source and the source's date. Where a thing could not be verified it says so. Nothing
here is from memory; where a search came back empty, that gap is recorded rather than
filled.

The short version. The two videos that did well replied to a claim people were already
arguing about, in a form they could see (a drink, a month, a word), and handed the viewer
a sentence they could repeat the same evening. The two that did badly explained a claim
nobody was arguing about that week, from a textbook rather than from a post, and gave the
viewer a position rather than a word. The rest of this document shows the evidence for
that reading, then proposes the format.

## 1. The cortisol trend

### What it is

There is no single trend called "cortisol reset". Searches on 10 November 2026 for that
exact phrase returned nothing under that name. What exists is a cluster of trends that
share one premise, that ordinary stress raises cortisol and that food, drink, supplements
or routines can bring it down: the "cortisol cocktail" (also "adrenal cocktail"), "cortisol
detox", "cortisol face", and in 2025 to 2026 the "cortisol spike" meme. The author's video
sat inside that cluster, and the cluster is the thing that spread.

The cortisol cocktail is a non-alcoholic drink, typically orange juice, coconut water,
sea salt and sometimes magnesium or cream of tartar, that influencers say lowers stress by
managing cortisol. It went viral on TikTok in 2023 and was still being covered as viral in
mid-2025 (NewsNation, https://www.newsnationnow.com/health/cortisol-cocktail-stress-tiktok/,
accessed 10 November 2026; Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortisol_cocktail,
accessed 10 November 2026). No original creator could be identified. Coverage names
several influencers who posted recipes but credits none with inventing it (Bustle,
https://www.bustle.com/wellness/adrenal-cocktail-tiktok; Spoon University,
https://spoonuniversity.com/news/tiktok-adrenal-cocktail/; both accessed 10 November 2026).
The claim behind it, "adrenal fatigue", is not a recognised medical condition (Wikipedia,
as above).

### How big it is

One report put the #cortisoldetox hashtag at nearly 800 million views on TikTok
(familienservice, https://en.familienservice.de/-/cortisol-detox-trend, accessed 10 November
2026). CNN described the cocktail as having "amassed millions of views" (CNN, 1 August
2025, https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/01/health/cortisol-cocktail-stress-relief-adrenal-wellness).
WWD reported in January 2025 that cortisol-lowering supplements were gaining traction on
TikTok alongside the mocktails (WWD,
https://wwd.com/beauty-industry-news/wellness/cortisol-face-mocktail-supplement-tiktok-trend-1236774663/).
Medscape reported in April 2025 that physicians were seeing patients ask for cortisol
testing after influencer content (Medscape,
https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/behind-cortisol-trend-misinformation-could-drive-unnecessary-2025a100090t).
The Guardian's 2024 year-end wellness list already included "cortisol face" (via AOL,
https://www.aol.com/lifestyle/biggest-wellness-trends-2024-far-160039700.html, accessed 10
November 2026).

The trend did not fade in 2026. It became a meme. The phrase "spike my cortisol" first
appeared on X on 27 February 2025 and peaked in early March 2025 (Daily Dot,
https://dailydot.com/spike-my-cortisol, accessed 10 November 2026). Through 2025 it became a
reaction format paired with a "cortisol level" meter graphic running from low to high, and
by January 2026 it had crossed into looksmaxxing communities (Know Your Meme,
https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/cortisol-level-cortisol-spike, accessed 10 November 2026;
Hola, 9 April 2026, https://www.hola.com/us/lifestyle/20260409894734/cortisol-spike-explained/).
Mainstream outlets were still running explainers in 2026: the Associated Press on 2
February 2026 (via PBS NewsHour,
https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/do-you-need-to-control-your-cortisol-levels-probably-not-doctors-say)
and the Cleveland Clinic on 12 May 2026
(https://newsroom.clevelandclinic.org/2026/05/12/what-some-people-get-wrong-about-cortisol).

Two signals of scale could not be verified and should not be repeated as fact: a trend
site's claim that searches for "cortisol meter" rose from 30 a month in April 2025 to
9,900 in March 2026 (https://www.risingtrends.co/blog/cortisol-meter-trend), and a
packaging-industry claim that "cortisol" searches nearly doubled in 2026
(https://www.bakeryandsnacks.com/Article/2026/07/22/cortisol-the-viral-trend-thats-rewriting-shopping-lists/).
Both are marketing-adjacent.

### What the counter-content looks like

The counter-content is unusually consistent, and it comes from endocrinologists rather than
from psychologists. Stanford endocrinologist Marilyn Tan told CNBC: "I'm not aware of any
studies that looked at the effects of these individual ingredients on cortisol levels"
(CNBC, 5 July 2025,
https://www.cnbc.com/2025/07/05/can-the-viral-cortisol-cocktail-reduce-stress-doctors-weigh-in.html).
Cleveland Clinic endocrinologist Pratibha Rao said there is no evidence the drink supports
the adrenal glands beyond a healthy diet (Cleveland Clinic,
https://health.clevelandclinic.org/what-to-know-about-adrenal-cocktails, accessed 10
November 2026). A Case Western dietitian called it "essentially an electrolyte drink, not a
hormone treatment" (Case Western Reserve University,
https://case.edu/news/nutritions-lindsay-malone-says-cortisol-cocktails-lack-evidence-lowering-cortisol,
accessed 10 November 2026). The Washington Post ran "What people get wrong about cortisol"
on 17 February 2025 (https://www.washingtonpost.com/wellness/2025/02/17/cortisol-levels-high/).
A UK endocrinologist's point, that the trend "conflates high stress with high cortisol", is
the one most useful to a psychologist (Dazed,
https://www.dazeddigital.com/beauty/article/63063/1/the-truth-behind-tiktok-cortisol-obsession-hormone-balancing-viral,
accessed 10 November 2026).

Note the shape of every one of these corrections: one sentence, repeatable, and it concedes
the harmless part ("generally harmless for healthy people", CNBC and Cleveland Clinic as
above). That is the register the author's video shared.

### Did debunk-style videos on it also do well

No per-video view counts for a named clinician's cortisol debunk could be found. Searches
for Karan Rajan or "Dr Idz" cortisol videos returned no coverage of a specific clip. What
was found is indirect but telling: a cortisol supplement advertiser used an AI deepfake of
Dr Karan Rajan to sell its product on TikTok Shop, which he exposed in 2025 (EBU,
https://spotlight.ebu.ch/p/deepfake-epidemic-doctor-finds-himself, accessed 10 November
2026). A seller only steals a debunker's face when the debunker's face carries more trust
with that audience than the seller's own.

The research on debunking formats is more useful than the view counts. An online
experiment with 873 participants found that most correction videos reduced false belief,
that expert "didactic" corrections gained more from fast background music than lay
testimonials did, and that the gain worked by suppressing counter-arguing, not by better
encoding (Li, Li and Yang, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, September 2024,
https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/5/zmae007/7729237). A summary of that work notes
that videos by medical professionals drew more engagement than those by non-experts
(Institute for Public Relations,
https://instituteforpr.org/should-experts-or-laypeople-debunk-misinformation-on-tiktok,
accessed 10 November 2026). A CHI 2026 study of 1,649 duet and stitch debunking videos from
14 health professionals, with 420 survey and 20 interview participants, found the
professionals were seen as credible and that exposure to their debunks significantly
reduced belief in the original misinformation (ACM,
https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790857, accessed 10 November 2026).

Two cautions sit beside that. The same group's earlier interviews with debunking clinicians
found that "the perception of engagement drowns correction signals": the original claim's
view count outruns the correction's (arXiv, December 2024,
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04999). And a 2026 Romanian study of stitch debunks found that
informative corrections were most common but humour and sarcasm drew the most engagement,
"a tension between informational clarity and platform visibility" (Romanian Journal of
Communication and Public Relations, vol. 28 no. 1, 2026,
https://www.journalofcommunication.ro/index.php/journalofcommunication/article/view/892).
The author does not do sarcasm. The lesson for her is not to add it but to find the other
thing that competes with it, which is timing and precision; see section 6.

## 2. November theory

### Origin and claim

"November theory" is the November instalment of TikTok's "month theory" series, which
began with "October theory". October theory holds that 1 October is a second New Year, a
fresh start for goals, habits and relationships. It first appeared on TikTok in 2023 and
took off in late September and October 2024; creator Macy Morgan is often credited with
the early posts, and a video by Chloe Van Berkel posted on 2 October had 1.7 million views
(Daily Dot, https://www.dailydot.com/memes/october-theory-tiktok/, accessed 10 November
2026; Baylor Lariat, 15 October 2024,
https://baylorlariat.com/2024/10/15/october-theory-dont-let-change-spook-you/; Calm,
https://www.calm.com/blog/october-theory, accessed 10 November 2026). There is no founder
and no rule list (Camille Styles,
https://camillestyles.com/wellness/health/healthy-habits/october-theory-explained/, accessed
10 November 2026).

November theory moved the idea to relationships. Vice describes it as the belief that
November, as the start of the holiday season, is the month a relationship is either dropped
or deepened, and ties it to cuffing season: "some attempt to tie down their current
situationships or talking stages, while others go out on the prowl for a more serious
connection". Matchmaker Susan Trombetti is quoted saying the holiday "test" has been talked
about for years (Vice,
https://www.vice.com/en/article/what-is-the-november-theory-the-dating-trend-that-could-make-or-break-your-relationship/,
accessed 10 November 2026; publication date not shown in search results). The TikTok
discover pages for the term tag it #fall25 and describe it as "the belief that November
brings clarity, fate, and change, especially in love" (TikTok,
https://www.tiktok.com/discover/november-theory-relationship and
https://www.tiktok.com/@thaliaspoetry/video/7568545192484162871, accessed 10 November 2026).
A Spanish-language variant ties it to the number 11 and Mercury retrograde and claims exes
return in November (TikTok, https://www.tiktok.com/discover/teoria-de-noviembre-novios,
accessed 10 November 2026).

### When it peaked and how creators used it

The trend is seasonal and recurs. It ran in November 2025 (the #fall25 tagging above) and
is running again now: a November 2026 trend round-up lists it, a dating blog has 2026
"November theory" and "December theory" pages, and a Thanksgiving variant says whoever you
bring home is the relationship that has "crossed from private to real" (New Engen,
https://newengen.com/insights/tiktok-trends/, accessed 10 November 2026; NexSpark,
https://nexspark.com/blog/november-theory-dating, accessed 10 November 2026). One source
states plainly that no research has looked at November theory (NexSpark, as above). That
is right: the nearest real finding is the "fresh start effect", three archival studies in
which diet searches, gym visits and goal commitments rose after temporal landmarks such as
the start of a month (Dai, Milkman and Riis, Management Science 60(10), 2014,
https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf).

Creators used it in three ways: as a horoscope ("if it ends, it served its purpose"), as a
dating deadline (the Thanksgiving test), and as a frame for naming what they were in
(situationship, talking stage, limerence). The author's video belonged to the third kind,
and the third kind is the one that gives the viewer a word.

## 3. Why "situationship" and "limerence" draw views

Both words name a state people were already in and had no word for. "Situationship" is
defined by Merriam-Webster's slang page as "when you're more than friends but less than
official" (https://www.merriam-webster.com/slang/situationship, last updated 23 January
2025). It was a finalist for Oxford's 2023 Word of the Year, losing to "rizz" (CBS News,
https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rizz-oxford-dictionary-word-of-the-year-2023/, accessed 10
November 2026). Axios reported in February 2024 that the status was on the rise among Gen Z
and had reached candy packaging (Axios, 2 February 2024,
https://www.axios.com/2024/02/02/dating-culture-valentines-day-gen-z-situationships). The
date Merriam-Webster added it could not be confirmed from a primary source; secondary
sources say September 2023.

"Limerence" was coined by Dorothy Tennov in her 1979 book Love and Limerence for an
attachment marked by acute longing and intrusive thoughts (Wikipedia,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerence, accessed 10 November 2026). Its second life began
on TikTok: Betches covered it as "TikTok's latest dating term" on 30 April 2024
(https://www.betches.com/article/relationships/what-is-limerence-tiktok-latest-dating-term-851948-20240430);
a University of Colorado piece said viewers "latched onto the vernacular to describe their
overwhelming emotional experiences"
(https://news.cuanschutz.edu/news-stories/limerence-vs.-crush-why-your-obsession-might-be-something-more-serious,
accessed 10 November 2026); Dazed called TikTok "ground zero" for the revival and said the
appeal is that a label makes love seem like something you can fix
(https://www.dazeddigital.com/life-culture/article/69466/1/limerence-is-bullshit-tiktok-reddit-dating-relationships-dorothy-tennov,
accessed 10 November 2026). It was still rising in 2026: Salon asked "why is everyone
talking about limerence" on 18 May 2026
(https://www.salon.com/2026/05/18/why-is-everyone-talking-about-limerence/) and YPulse noted
on 13 August 2026 that "social media has brought back the term"
(https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2026/08/13/social-media-has-brought-back-the-term-limerence/).
Limerence is not a DSM diagnosis, and a 2025 content study of therapy-speak on TikTok
found "a disconcerting amount of misinformation and misrepresentation" of such terms
(ResearchGate,
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394948908_The_Use_of_Therapy-Speak_Throughout_Tik_Tok_Accuracy_of_Information_and_Sentiment,
accessed 10 November 2026). That misuse is the author's opening, not her obstacle: she is
one of the few people who can say what Tennov actually described.

Why a word travels is better understood than why a trend does. In four studies with 2,559
participants, perceived self-relevance and social relevance of content predicted the
intention to share it, with fMRI and field evidence that the two motives are causal (Cosme
et al., PNAS Nexus 4(2), 2025,
https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf019/7974719). A word for one's own state
is self-relevance at its most compact. There is also a clinical reason people feel relief
when they get the word: in Lieberman's 2007 fMRI study, labelling a negative image with an
emotion word reduced amygdala response and raised right ventrolateral prefrontal activity,
and labelling it with a name did not (Lieberman et al., Psychological Science, 2007,
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/). It is one study, and the authors say "may", but
it is the finding the author would cite in the book's own register: the word is the first
intervention.

## 4. Which formats hold attention in 2025 to 2026

### What the platforms say and do not say

Neither TikTok nor YouTube publishes retention benchmarks. YouTube Studio reports, for each
Short, the share of feed impressions that "stayed to watch" versus "swiped away"; the
figure is per video and there is no official threshold (Creator Essentials glossary,
https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/, accessed 10 November
2026; third-party, as no YouTube Help page surfaced). TikTok's Creator Academy is quoted
second-hand saying "completion rate is one of the strongest signals for content quality"
(Hootsuite, https://blog.hootsuite.com/tiktok-algorithm/, accessed 10 November 2026). Every
numerical claim about "the first three seconds" found in this search came from vendors, and
the vendors disagree (one second, three, four, five). Treat them as folklore. The author
should read her own "stayed to watch" figure after each upload instead.

One peer-reviewed finding bears directly on her evidence-first episodes. In a 2 x 2
experiment with 179 students, plain-language science videos were rated more credible and
raised viewers' confidence in their own ability to judge the study, and a warning video
shown beforehand did not stop the effect (Salzmann, Walther and Kaspar, Frontiers in
Psychology, 2 July 2025,
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1584695/full).
Simplicity buys credibility whether or not the content deserves it. Her competitors use
that. She can use it honestly by making the true sentence the simple one.

### What the formats are and who uses them

A 2025 analysis of 398 mental health TikToks found health professionals mainly used
talking-head explainers, enactments and stitches, while lived-experience creators used
compilations and "watch as I do this"; green-screen backgrounds appeared in 12% of
wellness influencers' videos and 6% of professionals' (Christiansen et al., Health
Expectations 28(3), 2025, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12021991/). Both
professionals and wellness influencers used marketing calls to action, which the authors
read as the commercialisation of mental health solutions (same source). A separate 2025
content analysis of #mentalhealth videos found advice or information in 32.9%, with "hidden
signs of OCD" as a typical example (Turuba et al., Digital Health, 2025,
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20552076241297062).

The feed itself favours this content. A Washington Post analysis of nearly 900 US users'
histories, published mid-December 2025, found TikTok's algorithm serves more mental health
content after one view and makes it harder to leave than politics, cats or Taylor Swift
(via Philadelphia Inquirer, 17 December 2025,
https://www.inquirer.com/health/tiktok-mental-health-content-rabbit-hole-20251217.html; and
FlowingData, 16 December 2025,
https://flowingdata.com/2025/12/16/getting-stuck-in-the-mental-health-section-of-the-tiktok-algorithm/).
Her distribution problem is therefore not reach. It is the first ten seconds.

On the specific forms asked about:

The stitch and the duet. These are the native reply forms, and the debunking evidence
above is about them (Li, Li and Yang 2024; ACM 2026; Romanian Journal 2026, all cited in
section 1). The stitch places the claim first and the reply second; the duet places them
side by side. One study found that the lived-experience duet, side by side, did more to
reduce stigma among adolescents than sequential stitches did (via ACM search summary,
https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790857, accessed 10 November 2026; authors and
venue of the adolescent study not confirmed). The author's "never a takedown" rule argues
against stitching a named creator's face. The same effect is had by stitching the trend
itself: the hashtag page, the discover page, the product listing, the recipe card.

Green screen over a post. The post is the visible object that makes a textbook claim a
live one. Professionals use it less than wellness creators (6% vs 12%, Christiansen 2025),
which means the field is open.

"Things I wish I knew as a psychologist" and "signs you". List forms. No performance data by
format was found in peer-reviewed work; the Turuba study records how common the "signs"
form is, not how it performs. The author should be careful with "signs you": it is the form
behind the self-diagnosis complaint clinicians make (The Mighty,
https://themighty.com/topic/mental-health/therapist-tiktok-diagnosis-trend/, accessed 10
November 2026).

POV. A text-on-screen form ("POV: you told your therapist your real thoughts") documented in
a January 2025 qualitative study (ScienceDirect,
https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S1438887126004905, accessed 10
November 2026). It is the identification form: the viewer is cast in the first word.

Myth versus fact split screen, and "rating trends as a therapist". No research or trade
coverage specific to these was found. They are variants of the duet and the list.

### The most-followed clinicians, and what could and could not be verified

Follower counts from third-party lists, with the caveat that they conflict and move:
clinical psychologist Dr Julie Smith, 4.9 million on TikTok, with her own site also listing

https://www.itv.com/thismorning/articles/debunking-health-myths-with-social-media-sensation-dr-karan,

What their top videos do right now could not be verified from search. The only specific

secret or a mechanism, not a topic; and the object in frame (rice grains, a brain, a
patient's sentence) does the work the textbook would have done.

## 5. The two that did badly, read against the two that did well

### Sleep

"You need eight hours" is a textbook sentence. In November 2026 nobody on the feed is
arguing about it in that form. They are arguing about "sleepmaxxing", mouth tape, nostril
expanders and "sleepy girl" mocktails, a trend AFP described on 7 August 2025 as generating
tens of millions of posts, with influencers selling tape, powders and melatonin gummies
(France 24,
https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20250807-dangerous-dreams-inside-internet-s-sleepmaxxing-craze;
National Geographic, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/does-sleepmaxxing-work,
accessed 10 November 2026). The one live argument about the number itself is a viral claim
that "eight hours" was invented by a mattress company in 1938, which Matthew Walker
rebutted on X, tracing "eight hours' rest" to Robert Owen in 1817 and citing a
meta-analysis placing lowest mortality at seven to eight hours (X,
https://x.com/sleepdiplomat/status/2041141432212136199, accessed 10 November 2026; date not
shown). The long-running dispute behind that, Alexey Guzey's 2019 critique that Walker
insists on eight when the National Sleep Foundation says seven to nine, is also a reply to
something visible, a book (LessWrong,
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/rJK96E83k2wbL6sz6/matthew-walker-s-why-we-sleep-is-riddled-with-scientific-and,
accessed 10 November 2026).

So the sleep video had the wrong opponent. It answered a claim from a textbook when the
audience was arguing about a roll of tape and a mattress company. Its repeatable sentence,
"seven to nine, and eight is the midpoint", existed but came at the end.

### Dopamine detox

"Dopamine fasting" dates from 2019 and has been debunked continuously since; the concept is
"not clearly defined" and critics call it unscientific (Wikipedia,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopamine_fasting, accessed 10 November 2026). In 2025 it was
still being debunked in general outlets (Rolling Out, 24 May 2025,
https://rollingout.com/2025/05/24/dopamine-detox-myth-neuroscience/; Medical Xpress, October
2025, https://medicalxpress.com/news/2025-10-dopamine-detox.html). The one real 2025 finding
nearby, a PNAS Nexus trial that blocked mobile internet on 467 phones for two weeks, was
framed by its authors as stimulus control, not dopamine (via Leave Me Alone,
https://leavemealone.com/blog/dopamine-detox/, accessed 10 November 2026; secondary). No
evidence of a 2025 to 2026 surge in the trend was found; the term's successor, the
"dopamine menu", was described as a 2024 trend (Chuckling Goat,
https://www.chucklinggoat.co.uk/dopamineandcortisol/, accessed 10 November 2026; weak
source). The video arrived after the argument had moved on, with no object in frame, and
its verdict ("you cannot detox a neurotransmitter") was the same sentence every prior
debunk had used.

### The mechanism, stated

Six variables, with the four videos read against each.

(a) Was the audience already arguing about the claim. Cortisol: yes, with endocrinologists
on one side and TikTok Shop on the other, and a meme running in parallel through 2025 and
2026. November theory: yes, every November, by design, and it was November. Sleep: not in
the form she answered. Dopamine detox: not any more.

(b) Time to the first surprising sentence. The cortisol and November videos could open on
a true thing the viewer did not expect ("no study has tested the drink"; "the real finding
is about gym visits in a 2014 paper"). The sleep and dopamine videos, built evidence-first,
reached their surprise after the evidence, which on a feed is after the swipe. The Frontiers
study above says the plain early sentence also buys credibility (Salzmann et al., 2025).

(c) Could the viewer identify themselves in the first five seconds. "You have been drinking
this" and "you are in one of these in November" are second-person states. "You need eight
hours" is a second-person instruction, which is a different thing: it casts the viewer as
the one being corrected. The self-relevance work (Cosme et al., 2025) predicts sharing for
the first kind and not the second.

(d) Did the video give them a word or a verdict they could repeat. Cortisol: "it's an
electrolyte drink", "stress is not cortisol". November: "situationship", "limerence",
"fresh start effect". Sleep: a range, which is not a sentence. Dopamine: a negation
everyone had heard. Lieberman's finding (2007) is why the word lands: naming is itself
relief.

(e) Did it react to something visible. A drink, a month, a word versus a textbook claim and
a concept. The formats that hold, stitch, duet, green screen over a post, all put the
visible thing first (Christiansen et al., 2025; ACM 2026). The sleep video had a roll of
mouth tape available and did not use it.

(f) Length. The two that worked were short and vertical. The two that did not were careful
and long, and carefulness is not what failed; the order did. The six-beat long form in
`FORMAT.md` already fixes this for the fifteen-minute episode by putting the verdict at
0:10. The short form needs the same discipline compressed to ten seconds.

In one paragraph: a psychology video spreads when it enters an argument the viewer is
already in, shows the object of that argument in the first second, says the one true
surprising thing by the third, lets the viewer see themselves by the fifth, and hands them
a word or a one-line verdict they can say at dinner. Precision and timing do the work that
sarcasm does for everyone else. Evidence still goes on screen under every sentence; it
simply goes second.

## 6. The format

Two lengths, one trend per week, same trend in both. The vertical is published first and
points to the horizontal. Both keep the series discipline from `BRIEF.md` and `FORMAT.md`:
every fact sourced and the source on screen, the composite declared, the real conditions
named and referred, the book quoted only in its printed words, never a takedown, and the
episode ends on the better clinical answer.

### The sixty-second vertical reply

Reply to the trend, never to a person. The visible object is the trend's own artefact: the
drink being poured, the hashtag page with its count, the discover page, the product
listing, the recipe card, the search bar typing the phrase. No other creator's face. If a
stitch is used, stitch the discover page or a brand's own ad, which are public claims, not
people.

Structure, with seconds:

0 to 10. The template below, word for word.

10 to 25. The one mechanism. One sentence of what the claim gets right, one of where it
breaks, one of what is true underneath. Each sentence carries its source in the mono band.
Three sources at most.

25 to 40. The viewer's own body or day. The jaw check, the morning timing, the count of
Novembers this has happened. Under fifteen seconds, done on camera at the same time.

40 to 52. The word or the verdict, said once, plainly, then repeated once as the on-screen
card. "It's an electrolyte drink." "Tennov's word for this was limerence, and she meant
something narrower." "The real finding is about the first of the month, not about love."

52 to 60. "If this is more than a bad week, see your doctor; the condition it can hide is
[named]. The longer version, with every source, is on the channel." End card.

The first ten seconds, as a template, word for word. Brackets are the slots; everything
else is spoken as written. Seconds are approximate; the test is the "stayed to watch"
figure, not the stopwatch.

0:00 to 0:02. The artefact fills the frame. Mono caption, the trend's own words in quotes,
no creator named. Spoken: "This is supposed to [the claim in the trend's own words]."

0:02 to 0:06. Cut to the author, or keep the artefact and add her voice. Spoken: "[The one
true sentence the viewer did not expect, with its number or its date]. The source is on
your screen." Mono band: author, outlet or journal, date.

0:06 to 0:10. Spoken: "I'm a clinical psychologist. The trend is right about one thing,
and it's wrong about one word. Here's the word."

Worked once, for cortisol. 0:00 "This is supposed to lower your cortisol." 0:02 "No study
has tested whether it does. The Stanford endocrinologist who looked for one couldn't find
it. The source is on your screen." Band: Marilyn Tan, CNBC, 5 July 2025. 0:06 "I'm a
clinical psychologist. The trend is right about one thing, and it's wrong about one word.
Here's the word." Then 0:10 onward: the word is "cortisol"; what you feel is stress, and
stress is real; the drink is an electrolyte drink (Case Western, 2026); here is what
cortisol actually does by the minute (AP via PBS, 2 February 2026); and the condition that
does round a face is Cushing's, which a doctor tests for (Healthline, accessed 10 November
2026).

Worked once, for November theory. 0:00 "November is supposed to decide your relationship."
0:02 "The only study near this found people search for diets and go to the gym more after
the first of any month. It was 2014 and it was not about love. The source is on your
screen." Band: Dai, Milkman and Riis, Management Science, 2014. 0:06 "I'm a clinical
psychologist. The trend is right about one thing, and it's wrong about one word. Here's the
word." Then: the word is "theory"; the true thing is the fresh start; and the word the
viewer may actually need is the one Tennov coined in 1979, said precisely.

What never goes in the first ten seconds: a question, a greeting, the channel name, "you
need", "everything you know is wrong", any number the sources do not carry. These are the
`SEASON.md` hook rules, unchanged.

### The six-to-eight-minute horizontal

The same trend, the same week, the full chain of custody. This is the six-beat form in
`FORMAT.md` compressed by half: the sentence, the verdict, the commitment, two stories
instead of three, the room, the return. The cold open is the vertical's first ten seconds
played again, so a viewer who came from the short recognises it and a viewer who did not
gets the same entry.

0:00 to 0:10. The ten-second template, identical to the vertical.

0:10 to 0:30. The verdict as a headline, twelve words or fewer on the card, in the form
"the evidence is weaker than the claim, and here is what is solid underneath". The author
introduces herself in one sentence. The book is on the desk, closed.

0:30 to 0:50. The commitment. "Before I show you why, finish this sentence. I would stop
believing this if..." Ten seconds of silence, mono clock. This is the series' own device
and it is kept.

0:50 to 4:30. Two stories, not three. Where the sentence was born: the real finding it
started as, with a person, a year and a place (for cortisol, the Cushing's literature and
the "adrenal fatigue" coinage; for November theory, the 2014 fresh start paper). Where it
broke: the hand-off that stretched it, and who gained, named without contempt (the
supplement market, WWD, January 2025; the TikTok Shop ad that borrowed a doctor's face,
EBU, 2025; the dating sites publishing a "theory" page for every month, NexSpark, 2026).
Two or three sources per story, on screen under every factual sentence. Six sources in
all.

4:30 to 6:30. The room. The composite, declared as a composite, sorted with the book's
three questions in their printed words. The thing the viewer does, on their own body or
their own day, done with the host in real time. The real conditions named and referred
here. The beat ends on the better clinical answer.

6:30 to 7:30. The return. "Look at what you wrote." Then the retraction line, first
person, specific enough that a researcher could bring the evidence. Then: references in
the description, transcript on the site, see a doctor for the symptoms. End card.

Length is held at six to eight minutes by cutting a story, never by cutting the sources
from under a sentence.

### What to measure

After each upload: the "stayed to watch" share on the Short, and the retention curve at
0:10 and 0:30 on the horizontal. If the Short's early share is weak, the fault is in the
first six seconds, and the fix is the artefact or the true sentence, not the thesis. If
the horizontal loses viewers at the commitment beat, hold it anyway for a month and read
it against episode 01, as `FORMAT.md` already requires.
