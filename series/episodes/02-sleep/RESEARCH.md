## 1. The claim as patients bring it

"I'm supposed to get eight hours and I never do, so I've given up." "I'm fine on five or six in the week, I catch up on Saturday." **"My watch says I only got four hours of deep sleep, so no wonder I feel like this."** "The book says the shorter you sleep the shorter you live, and now I lie there doing the maths." The third sentence is the cold-open card.

## 2. The kernel of good science

Two processes regulate sleep. A homeostatic pressure, Process S, builds during waking and discharges during sleep, read in the EEG as slow-wave activity; a circadian process, Process C, set by the body clock, gates when sleep is likely (Borbély, 1982). The drawing's gauge is Process S.

Restriction accumulates. Healthy adults held to four or six hours in bed for fourteen nights showed cumulative, dose-dependent deficits on every task; two weeks at six hours matched up to two nights without sleep. Their sleepiness ratings rose at first and then barely moved; in the authors' words, subjects were largely unaware of these increasing deficits (Van Dongen et al., 2003).

Need varies between people more than one number allows. The same laboratory found 58 percent of the variance in impairment under sleep loss stable and trait-like, not explained by sleep history (Van Dongen et al., 2004). The professional consensus is a floor, not a figure: seven or more hours for adults aged 18 to 60, and more than nine may suit some (Watson et al., 2015).

Duration and health. Across 16 prospective studies and 1.38 million people, self-reported short sleep went with a 12 percent higher risk of death and long sleep with 30 percent higher, a U shape (Cappuccio et al., 2010). The authors say it is not causal and that long sleep may mark illness already present. Short sleep was defined in the included studies as five hours or under, six or under, or under seven, and in one as four or under; long as over eight, nine or more, ten or more, or twelve or more; the reference was seven hours in most, so no single hour can be quoted as the paper's threshold [narrowed: the Methods were read in excerpt and the cut-offs vary by study].

Recovery restores some things and not others. After seven nights at three, five or seven hours, three nights at eight left the five- and seven-hour groups still below baseline on vigilance (Belenky et al., 2003). After five nights at five hours, a free weekend recovered about an hour of sleep and did not prevent the fall in insulin sensitivity once short nights resumed; circadian phase stayed delayed (Depner et al., 2019) [narrowed: the abstract says "circadian phase was delayed"; the melatonin measure behind it was not seen].

## 3. The overextension

Where eight hours came from. No study sets it. The consensus gives a floor of seven, and in three pre-industrial societies without electricity, adults slept 5.7 to 7.1 hours by actigraphy (Yetish et al., 2015). The only firm eight in the record is Robert Owen's labour slogan, eight hours' labour, recreation and rest, and rest there meant time off. Eight is a range rounded.

Whether a person can judge their own impairment. Under two weeks of six-hour nights, performance kept falling while felt sleepiness plateaued (Van Dongen et al., 2003). The patient who says she is fine on six may be right about how she feels and wrong about how she is doing.

What catching up restores. A long weekend sleep drains the gauge partway: sleepiness and some vigilance come back quickly. Not back by Monday are the rest of the deficit after moderate restriction (Belenky et al., 2003) and the metabolic shift and circadian delay (Depner et al., 2019). In the lie-in's favour: in 43,880 Swedes followed thirteen years, short weekday sleep with long weekend sleep carried no excess mortality under 65, while short sleep on both did (Åkerstedt et al., 2019). Observational, consistent with partial repayment, silent on mechanism.

## 4. The commercial edge

Consumer trackers. Against laboratory polysomnography, seven consumer devices detected sleep well and wake poorly, epoch sensitivity at or above 0.93 and specificity 0.18 to 0.54, and their sleep staging was inconsistent (Chinoy et al., 2021). The wrist measures time asleep fairly and its architecture poorly; "four hours of deep sleep" is the part the device is worst at.

Orthosomnia. Sleep physicians in Chicago described patients who arrived with tracker data, spent more time in bed to raise the score, and resisted a diagnosis the device disagreed with; they coined the word on the model of orthorexia (Baron et al., 2017). A case series of three, not a diagnosis.

The book. *Why We Sleep* (Walker, 2017) is the decade's best-selling sleep book. Chapter one prints "the shorter your sleep, the shorter your life span", says routinely sleeping under six or seven hours more than doubles cancer risk, says two-thirds of adults in developed nations fail to get "the recommended eight hours", and says the World Health Organization "has now declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout industrialized nations" [narrowed: the sentences are confirmed as the book's text from reproduced excerpts; the page number 4 was not checked against a copy, and the book's phrase is "throughout industrialized nations", not "global"]. Alexey Guzey, an independent researcher with a background in economics as Wikipedia describes him, neither sleep scientist nor peer reviewer, published a blog essay on 15 November 2019 checking chapter one's citations against their sources (Guzey, 2019) [narrowed: his description rests on Wikipedia and secondary accounts; his site could not be reached]. On the mortality line he points to the U shape; on the WHO line, Wikipedia records that the organisation denied the declaration and that Walker conceded in January 2020 that he had misremembered a 2014 CDC statement [narrowed: the denial and the concession rest on Wikipedia, which cites BBC Radio 4's *More or Less* of 25 January 2020; neither was seen or heard]. The essay is a reading of footnotes, not a study; say so on screen. Who gained: an author, a publisher, and behind them an industry selling the fear a round number creates.

## 5. Real conditions that must be named

Insomnia disorder is diagnosed by clinical interview, questionnaires and a sleep diary, not by a tracker, and actigraphy is not routine. First-line treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, in person or digital (Riemann et al., 2023), which the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends strongly while advising against sleep hygiene on its own (Edinger et al., 2021).

Obstructive sleep apnoea is diagnosed by polysomnography or a technically adequate home sleep apnoea test ordered by a clinician; questionnaires and prediction tools alone are not sufficient (Kapur et al., 2017). Loud snoring, witnessed pauses and unrefreshing sleep despite enough hours are a referral, not a debt.

## 6. The verdict

The evidence is weaker than the claim: eight hours is an average, not a rule; a weekend repays part of a week; what is solid is that sleep debt accumulates and people stop feeling it.

Card: Eight hours is an average, not a rule; sleep debt accumulates.

## 7. What is genuinely open

Whether the long-sleep arm of the U is anything but a marker of illness already present. No trial can lengthen sleep to find out, and the cohorts rest on self-report.

How much of weekend recovery is real repayment. The laboratory says metabolism and timing are not restored; the Swedish cohort says mortality may be. No study bridges them.

Whether one person's sleep need can be measured rather than estimated from a population.

## 9. The thing the viewer does

Option A, the seven-night count. The host names each of the last seven nights in turn, and the viewer says aloud, with her, the hours they got and the hours they would have wanted. Fourteen numbers, under a minute. The difference is the week's debt, and it means something because the episode is about a gauge that does not empty. It cannot be failed: a zero is an answer, and so is a surplus.

Option B, the free-morning gap: the time the viewer woke on their last alarm-free morning against the weekday alarm. The gap is the pressure the week left behind. Under a minute, nothing to get wrong.

Recommend A. It covers the week, yields a number, is the count the composite does in the room, and sits under the verdict: the viewer watches the debt accumulate and notices they had not felt it.
