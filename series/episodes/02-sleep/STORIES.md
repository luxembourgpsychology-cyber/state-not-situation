## Slot one. Where the sentence was born

The honest finding of this pass is that "eight hours" has no single birth. It is older than sleep science, it was never the output of a measurement, and the two modern recommendations that people assume say eight do not say eight. That is itself the story, and each candidate below is one way of telling it. I rank them by how well documented they are and by how much they surprise, since the format needs both.

### Ranked first. The professor who could not change the length of his own day

Nathaniel Kleitman, University of Chicago, with his graduate student Bruce Richardson. Mammoth Cave, Kentucky, from 4 June 1938, for thirty-two days, about a hundred and forty feet underground, with no daylight and no clocks but their own.

The measurement at its centre: the two men lived a twenty-eight-hour day, nineteen hours awake and nine asleep, taking their body temperature on a fixed timetable. Over the month Richardson's temperature rhythm stretched to fit the new day [narrowed: every account agrees he adapted and Kleitman did not, and Kleitman put it down to age or individual variation; none seen says in which week it happened, so "within about two weeks" is not to be spoken]. Kleitman's never did; it kept to roughly twenty-four hours for the whole month, and he went on feeling sleepy at about ten in the evening. The cave was a constant 54 degrees Fahrenheit, and Richardson's age is given as 20 in one account and 25 in the Park Service's [narrowed: do not give his age]. Kleitman had founded what is usually called the first dedicated sleep laboratory, at Chicago, after joining its physiology department in 1925, and his 1939 book *Sleep and Wakefulness* became the field's reference [narrowed: the laboratory and the 1925 date come from the Scientist retrospective and the Park Service account, not from a primary record]. The historian Matthew Wolf-Meyer argues that this body of work helped to establish consolidated, regular sleep as a norm in the United States, which is the thread that connects the cave to the sentence.

The mechanism, for dinner: the clock is inside you, it runs at about a day, and two healthy people in the same cave can differ in how far it will bend.

What this story does for the episode: it sets the ground that sleep is a rhythm with individual differences before anyone has mentioned a number, so that when "eight hours" arrives in slot two it already looks like a round figure laid over a varying thing. It also gives the figure a scene instead of a statistic.

[Fact-check: Wolf-Meyer's DOI is https://doi.org/10.1111/anoc.12014, and its abstract confirms the article turns on the Mammoth Cave experiment; the Scientist's "Cave Dwellers, 1938" and the Park Service's "Dreaming underground" both say Kleitman published the cave results in *Sleep and Wakefulness* (1939), but the chapter and page were not seen, so the book is cited as the primary account on their word.] One caution: an online claim that Kleitman worked for a mattress company and falsified results circulates widely and is unsupported; it must not be mentioned even to deny it.

### Ranked second. Eighteen people in a room voted on a range, and nobody wrote down eight

The National Sleep Foundation's expert panel, chaired by Max Hirshkowitz, convened by the foundation and publishing in March 2015 [narrowed: neither the year the panel met nor where it sat was found; the foundation is based in Arlington, Virginia, and that is all the place the story can carry].

The decision at its centre: an eighteen-member multidisciplinary panel representing twelve stakeholder organisations (fact-check: confirmed in the paper's abstract) reviewed the literature and then used the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method, a formal voting procedure, to produce a sleep duration recommendation for each of nine age groups, each duration marked as recommended, may be appropriate, or not recommended. For adults from eighteen to sixty-four the recommended range came out at seven to nine hours. For older adults, seven to eight. In the same year a separate panel of fifteen experts convened by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society, using a modified RAND method over twelve months, recommended seven or more hours for adults aged eighteen to sixty and set no upper limit, saying it was uncertain whether more than nine was harmful.

The mechanism, for dinner: the modern recommendation is a vote on a range by a committee reading cohort studies, and its floor is seven; eight is the midpoint of a span, not a finding.

What this story does for the episode: it makes the hand-off in slot two land, because the best-selling book of 2017 says the foundation recommends eight, and the foundation's own paper says seven to nine. It also hands the viewer the number they will actually use in the room in beat five.

### Ranked third. The number was a demand for rest before it was a measurement of sleep

James Stephens and the stonemasons building the University of Melbourne, who put down their tools on 21 April 1856 and marched under the demand "eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest". The slogan is usually traced further back to Robert Owen in 1817, in Britain, who had already cut the day in his own mill at New Lanark.

The decision at its centre: a working day divided into three equal parts, the third of them rest. After months of negotiation the Melbourne masons won eight hours for the wages they had received for ten. The movement's "888" became a civic emblem in Victoria and the eight-hour day spread through the labour movements of the later nineteenth century.

The mechanism, for dinner: the third eight was arithmetic, twenty-four divided by three, and it was asked for by men laying stone, not measured by anyone watching people sleep.

Why it ranks third. The event is well documented by the National Museum of Australia, the University of Melbourne archives and Museums Victoria, and Owen's authorship of the slogan is repeated everywhere, but I found no source that documents the descent from the labour slogan to the sleep recommendation. The connection is plausible and widely asserted; it is not shown. Under the house rule that is a sentence the episode cannot say as fact. It can be said as "the number was in the air for a century before anyone measured sleep", which is true, and the fact-checker may find a historian who has traced the line (Wolf-Meyer's book *The Slumbering Masses*, 2012, is the first place to look). [Struck by the fact-checker: a sentence here attributed to Matthew Walker a 2026 social-media reply tracing "eight hours' rest" to Owen; no record of it was found and it is not to be mentioned.] [narrowed: Owen's 1817 formulation remains secondary; the National Museum of Australia says the idea was first proposed by Owen in 1817 at New Lanark but does not attach the slogan's exact words to him, and no primary or scholarly source for the wording was found. Say "usually traced to Robert Owen", not "Owen said".]

## Slot two. Where it broke

### Ranked first. The best-selling sleep book and the reader who checked chapter one

Matthew Walker, professor of neuroscience and psychology at the University of California, Berkeley, published *Why We Sleep* in 2017 and it became the sleep book of the decade. Alexey Guzey, an independent researcher with a background in economics [narrowed: Wikipedia's description; the essay itself was unreachable], published an essay on his own website on 15 November 2019 under the title "Matthew Walker's 'Why We Sleep' Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors". He examined chapter one, and said he had spent about a hundred and thirty hours on it to show how dense the problems were in a single chapter.

What he found, as the records describe it. The book says the National Sleep Foundation recommends eight hours; the foundation's 2015 paper says seven to nine, and the eight-hour figure drives the book's claim that two-thirds of adults fall short. The book says the World Health Organization "has now declared a sleep loss epidemic throughout industrialized nations"; no such declaration has been found, Wikipedia records the WHO denying it, and Guzey's own suggestion is that the source was a Centers for Disease Control page that had called insufficient sleep a public health epidemic and had changed its wording to "public health problem" before the book came out [narrowed: the book's sentence is confirmed from reproduced text; the denial rests on Wikipedia; the CDC explanation is Guzey's speculation as Shortform describes it, not a finding]. The book's sentence that the shorter your sleep the shorter your life sits on mortality data whose curve is lowest near seven hours and rises again on the long side, which the sentence leaves out; Guzey also noted that in the cardiovascular meta-analysis the book draws on, short sleep was not associated with total cardiovascular disease while long sleep was [narrowed: not found in any secondary account this pass; carry only if the essay itself shows it]. And one figure in the book reproduces a published graph with a bar removed, the bar that cut against the book's point. The graph is confirmed: it is from Milewski and colleagues' 2014 study of sports injuries by hours of sleep in 112 adolescent athletes, in the *Journal of Pediatric Orthopaedics*, and the bar removed was the left-most bar, the shortest-sleep group, which Gelman and Guzey say implies a monotonic relation the original did not show [narrowed: the figure's number in the book and what that bar showed in hours and injury rate were not seen; say "the shortest-sleep bar" and no number]. Guzey and the statistician Andrew Gelman wrote the graph up together as Gelman, A., & Guzey, A. (2020). Statistics as squid ink: How prominent researchers can get away with misrepresenting data. *Chance, 33*(2), 25–27. https://doi.org/10.1080/09332480.2020.1754069 [narrowed: the citation is from Wikipedia's reference list and a journal index, not from the publisher's page].

What Walker did and did not do. On 19 December 2019 he posted "Why We Sleep: Responses to questions from readers" on his blog [narrowed: the post was not read; its date, title and framing come from Guzey's and Shortform's descriptions and Wikipedia]. It is framed as answering readers, not the essay, which Guzey notes in his own words, and the accounts agree it did not take up most of the specific points. He gave a drowsy-driving figure of 15 to 20 percent of fatal crashes, citing a news article and an AAA Foundation report, where Guzey's figure concerned all crashes [narrowed: as Shortform reports both sides]. He mentioned a second edition correcting errors [narrowed: Shortform's report; the corrected edition was not seen]. The University of California, Berkeley, made no public finding that was found: Guzey's essay reports that the university's official response described the problems as minor, and Gelman's blog of 24 March 2020 reports that when a reader filed a research-misconduct complaint the university replied that the matter did not warrant further investigation [narrowed: "found no research misconduct by its definition" is not carried by any record seen; say only what Guzey and Gelman report, and attribute it to them]. Wikipedia reports that Walker later conceded the WHO claim was misremembered and came from a 2014 CDC statement, citing BBC Radio 4's *More or Less*, "Dozy Science", 25 January 2020 [narrowed: the broadcast was not heard; carry as "he has since said" with Wikipedia's citation, or confirm against the programme].

The label the format demands, in the script's own words: Guzey's essay is a blog post by a reader, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the episode says so the moment it is named. Who gained, without contempt: a book that gave millions of people a reason to take sleep seriously, and sold on the strength of a single number that was rounder than the evidence. That is the hand-off. The number got simpler each time it changed hands.

The mechanism, for dinner: a range of seven to nine became eight in the retelling, and once it was eight, two-thirds of the population were failing.

### Ranked second. Three patients whose complaint was a number on their wrist

Kelly Glazer Baron, then at Rush University Medical Center, with colleagues at Northwestern University's Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago. Published February 2017 in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, the same year as the book.

The measurement at its centre: a case series of three patients, named in the paper as Mr R, Ms B and Mr S, whose presenting complaint was their tracker's sleep score rather than how they felt. Ms B's overnight polysomnography showed a good deal of deep sleep; her reply, quoted in the paper, was "Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?" The authors named the pattern orthosomnia, by analogy with orthorexia, and recorded that these patients spent more time in bed to improve their numbers, which is the opposite of what the treatment for insomnia asks. They also noted that consumer trackers could not reliably distinguish sleep stages. Orthosomnia is a descriptive label and appears in no diagnostic manual, which the script must say.

Who gained, without contempt: the makers of wrist trackers, which gave people something to measure where before there had been only a feeling, and sold the measurement against a target of eight. The *New York Times* called sleep "the new status symbol" on 8 April 2017, in a piece by Penelope Green on the industry around it; that article is a scene-setter and a secondary source, not evidence.

The mechanism, for dinner: once sleep became a score, people treated a bad number as a bad night even when the laboratory said otherwise, and stayed in bed longer to fix it.

## Slot three. What is true underneath

### Ranked first. Six hours for two weeks, in Philadelphia

Hans Van Dongen, Greg Maislin, Janet Mullington and David Dinges, University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine, 2003. Forty-eight healthy adults aged twenty-one to thirty-eight lived in the laboratory under continuous monitoring.

The measurement at its centre: for fourteen consecutive nights the volunteers were allowed eight, six or four hours in bed, and a fourth group went without sleep for three nights. Performance was measured with the psychomotor vigilance task, which counts lapses, the moments when a person fails to respond to a light for half a second. Lapses rose steadily, night after night, in the six-hour and four-hour groups, and by the fourteenth day the six-hour group was lapsing at a rate the authors compared to up to two nights without any sleep at all [narrowed: the abstract says "equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation"; say "up to two", not "one to two"]. Across all four conditions the lapses tracked one quantity, the hours of wakefulness accumulated beyond about 15.84 a day. The ratings of sleepiness did something different: they jumped after the first short night and then barely moved [confirmed: the abstract says sleepiness showed "an acute response to sleep restriction but only small further increases on subsequent days"], and the six-hour and four-hour groups could not be told apart by how sleepy they said they felt [narrowed: this detail is not in the abstract and was not found in any record; it stays out of the script unless the full text shows it]. The abstract's sentence is confirmed word for word: "Sleepiness ratings suggest that subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits, which may explain why the impact of chronic sleep restriction on waking cognitive functions is often assumed to be benign."

The mechanism, for dinner: the debt kept growing every night, and the feeling of being tired stopped growing after the first one, so the people who were worst affected were the ones most certain they had adjusted.

This is the study the figure's drawing is for. It is also the thing the viewer does in beat five, counting the hours they are short this week, because the study is the reason a count means more than a feeling.

### Ranked second. The weekend that did not pay the debt back

Christopher Depner and Kenneth Wright, University of Colorado Boulder, with the study run at the Clinical Translational Research Center on the Anschutz Medical Campus in Aurora, Colorado. Published online in *Current Biology* on 28 February 2019.

The measurement at its centre: thirty-six healthy adults aged eighteen to thirty-nine lived in the laboratory. One group could sleep up to nine hours every night. One group was held to five hours throughout. The third was held to five hours for five nights, then given a weekend of sleeping as long as they liked, then held to five hours for two more nights. The weekend group gained only about sixty-six minutes of sleep on average across the weekend. In the group restricted throughout, whole-body insulin sensitivity fell by about thirteen per cent. In the weekend group, once restriction resumed, whole-body, liver and muscle insulin sensitivity was worse by nine to twenty-seven per cent against baseline [narrowed: the abstract gives the range across all three measures, not liver and muscle alone], which the authors had not expected [narrowed: the surprise is the university release's framing, not the abstract's]; they attributed part of it to the shift in timing, people staying up late and sleeping into the afternoon, the pattern called social jetlag. The weekend group also ate less at night during the weekend, and that gain disappeared when the short nights returned. Depner said they had seen no benefit in any metabolic outcome in the people who slept in. Wright said it was not yet known whether an occasional weekend lie-in helps someone who is short only a night or two a week, which the study did not test.

The mechanism, for dinner: a weekend lie-in bought about an hour of sleep and moved the body's clock, and the metabolism that had been running on five hours did not come back.

Caveats the script must carry: thirty-six people, one metabolic outcome set, and small groups, the restricted-throughout group being fourteen [narrowed: fourteen is in the abstract; the control group's size was not seen, so "eight" is not to be spoken]; the protocol ran nine nights in condition inside a laboratory stay the university's release calls two weeks [narrowed: both figures are consistent once baseline nights are counted, but the exact stay was not seen]. The study restricted sleep to five hours, not six; the claim says eight, so the episode should not imply this study tested the gap between seven and eight.

Ad libitum weekend recovery sleep fails to prevent metabolic dysregulation during a repeating pattern of insufficient sleep and weekend recovery sleep. *Current Biology, 29*(6), 957–967.e4. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069. The author list is to verify; the title, journal, volume, pages and DOI were confirmed in two records.

### Ranked third. The curve with its bottom near seven

Francesco Cappuccio, Lanfranco D'Elia, Pasquale Strazzullo and Michelle Miller, University of Warwick and the Federico II University of Naples, 2010. The measurement at its centre: a meta-analysis of sixteen prospective studies with twenty-seven cohorts, 1,382,999 people followed for four to twenty-five years and 112,566 deaths. Short sleepers, defined in the included studies as five hours or under, six or under, or under seven, and in one as four or under, had a pooled relative risk of death of 1.12; long sleepers, defined as over eight, nine or more, ten or more, or twelve or more, had 1.30, against a reference of seven hours in most studies [narrowed: the cut-offs are now stated from the Methods, read in excerpt; quote no single hour as the paper's own]. Both findings carried heterogeneity between studies. The paper describes rather than fits the U shape, and Cappuccio told the press at the time that short sleep may be a cause of ill health while long sleep is more likely a marker of it. Behind it stands the earlier and larger single cohort, Daniel Kripke's 2002 analysis of 1.1 million Americans in the Cancer Prevention Study II, where survival was best in people reporting about seven hours and the risk rose beyond eight and a half.

The mechanism, for dinner: in the biggest counts we have, the people who lived longest said they slept about seven, and the people who slept nine were in more trouble than the people who slept six, though nobody knows how much of that is illness making people sleep.

Why it ranks third: it is the firmest number in the episode and the one most people will remember, but it is an association, it is self-reported sleep, and it cannot be drawn on one body. It belongs as the sentence that closes slot three or opens the room, not as a story with a scene. It is also where Guzey's criticism of the book bites, so it ties slots two and three together.

### A fourth candidate, held in reserve: the man who proposed the two processes

Alexander Borbély, Institute of Pharmacology, University of Zurich, 1982. He proposed that sleep is governed by two processes, a homeostatic pressure (Process S) that rises through waking and falls through sleep, read from the slow-wave activity of the sleeping brain, and a circadian process (Process C) that is independent of how long one has been awake. He had presented an early version at Sounion in 1979 and named the two processes at the Budapest congress of 1980; the key experiment behind S was sleep deprivation in rats. The 1982 paper has been cited more than five thousand times and remains the frame in which Van Dongen's and Depner's results are read.

It is not ranked among the three because it is a model, not a measurement with a scene, and the format wants the measurement. But it is the vocabulary the drawing uses, and the script should name him once when the gauge first appears, with the source on screen. Borbély, A. A. (1982). A two process model of sleep regulation. *Human Neurobiology, 1*(3), 195–204. Borbély, A. A. (2022). The two-process model of sleep regulation: Beginnings and outlook. *Journal of Sleep Research, 31*(4), e13598. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13598 [Fact-check: confirmed, PMC9540767; the 2022 review itself gives Sounion 1979 and Budapest 1980 and names the rat deprivation study with Neuhaus as the key experiment; the 1982 paper is PMID 7185792 and the Zurich CV gives more than 5,000 citations].

Two further leads I checked and set aside. Yetish and colleagues (2015, *Current Biology, 25*, 2862–2868) measured sleep in the Hadza, San and Tsimane and found 5.7 to 7.1 hours a night, nearly an hour longer in winter; it is a strong reversal of expectation but it says nothing about what those hours cost or restore, so it would be interesting rather than load-bearing, and the format cuts interesting. Ekirch's 2001 argument that pre-industrial Europeans slept in two segments (*American Historical Review, 106*, 343–386) has been contested in 2023 and defended in 2024, and the episode should not open a historians' dispute it cannot close.

## The retraction line

"I would take this episode down if a controlled study held people at six hours for two weeks, let them sleep as they liked for two nights, and found their measured reaction times and their glucose handling back at baseline, or if a large prospective cohort with measured rather than reported sleep found the lowest mortality at eight hours rather than near seven."

A shorter form, if the pace needs it: "I would take this down if a controlled study showed two free nights after two weeks at six hours returned measured performance and metabolism to baseline."
