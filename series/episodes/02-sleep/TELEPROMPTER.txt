[3]
In June 1938 a professor from Chicago and his student went a hundred and forty feet into a cave in Kentucky to live a twenty-eight-hour day, and after a month the student could and the professor could not. The professor was Nathaniel Kleitman, who studied sleep at the University of Chicago. The student was Bruce Richardson. On the fourth of June they went into Mammoth Cave, with no daylight and a timetable of their own, and they stayed thirty-two days.

[4]
The day they set themselves was nineteen hours awake and nine asleep. Twenty-eight hours, not twenty-four. They took their body temperature on a fixed timetable, and that rise and fall was the measurement. Kleitman published the results in 1939, in Sleep and Wakefulness. Over the month, Richardson's temperature rhythm stretched to fit the longer day. Kleitman's never did. It kept to roughly twenty-four hours, and he went on feeling sleepy at about ten in the evening, in a cave that stayed at fifty-four degrees Fahrenheit.

[5]
Kleitman put the difference down to age, or to variation between people. The historian Matthew Wolf-Meyer, in 2013, argued that this body of work helped establish one long, regular block of sleep as the norm in the United States. So before anyone had a number, there was a rhythm. The clock is inside you, it runs at about a day, and two healthy people in the same cave can differ in how far it will bend. Nobody down there measured eight of anything.

[6]
The number arrived later, and it got rounder each time it changed hands. In 2017 Matthew Walker, a professor of neuroscience and psychology at the University of California, Berkeley, published Why We Sleep, and it's the sleep book people bring into my room. Its first chapter says that two-thirds of adults in developed nations fail to get "the recommended eight hours".

[7]
Now the recommendation that was in print when that book came out. The National Sleep Foundation convened a panel of eighteen people from twelve organisations and had them vote, by a formal method, on a range for each age group. Hirshkowitz and colleagues published the result in 2015: for adults from eighteen to sixty-four, seven to nine hours. Eight is the middle of a span a committee agreed on after reading the studies. The paper gives you the span. It doesn't give you the eight.

[8]
Who gained? An author, a publisher, and a great many readers who were handed a reason to take sleep seriously, which isn't a small thing. And the number they were handed was rounder than the evidence under it. A range of seven to nine became eight in the retelling, and once the number was eight, two-thirds of adults could be counted as falling short of it.

[9]
In Philadelphia, in 2003, healthy adults lived in a laboratory for fourteen nights and were allowed eight, six or four hours in bed. Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington and Dinges, at the University of Pennsylvania, ran it: forty-eight people in all, aged twenty-one to thirty-eight, counting a fourth group who went three nights without sleep. Each day a reaction test counted lapses, the moments a person fails to answer a light within half a second.

[10]
The lapses rose night after night in the six-hour group and the four-hour group. By day fourteen the six-hour group was lapsing at a rate the authors put as equivalent to up to two nights without sleep. Across all four groups, the lapses tracked one quantity: the hours awake beyond about sixteen a day, added up. Sleepiness did something else. It rose after the first short night, then showed only small further increases. Their sentence: "Sleepiness ratings suggest that subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits."

[11]
So the debt kept growing every night, and the feeling of being tired barely moved after the first one. Now the catching up. Belenky and colleagues, in 2003, gave sixty-six volunteers three, five, seven or nine hours in bed for seven nights, then eight hours for three nights. The nine-hour group stayed at baseline. The five-hour and seven-hour groups settled at a reduced level, and three nights at eight didn't bring them back to baseline. The three-hour group came up fast, and only to that same reduced level.

[12]
Then the weekend itself, in a laboratory. Depner and colleagues, in Colorado, in 2019, kept thirty-six healthy adults in the laboratory. One group could sleep up to nine hours a night. One was held to five throughout. The third was held to five for five nights, given a weekend of sleeping as long as they liked, then held to five again. The weekend bought them about an hour more sleep than baseline. It didn't test whether a lie-in helps someone who's short only a night or two a week.

[13]
Then the short nights came back, and the body hadn't reset. In the group held to five, whole-body insulin sensitivity fell about thirteen percent. In the weekend group, once the short nights resumed, insulin sensitivity across whole body, liver and muscle was down nine to twenty-seven percent, and their body clock had shifted later. The authors' conclusion: weekend recovery sleep is not an effective countermeasure. That's five hours, not six, in thirty-six people. A free weekend bought an hour and moved the clock, and the metabolism didn't come back.


[14]
The person I'm going to describe is a composite. She isn't one patient. She's several, with the details changed. She's in her forties. She sleeps about six hours on a weeknight and nine at the weekend, and she'll tell you the weekend covers it. She wears a watch, and this week it told her she'd had four hours of deep sleep, so no wonder she feels like this. She's decided she's a bad sleeper, and the measure she's failing is eight.

[15]
I don't start with the watch, and I don't start with the eight. I can't check the watch's number. Chinoy and colleagues, in 2021, tested seven consumer devices against the sleep laboratory: they detected sleep well, detected wake poorly, and their staging of sleep was inconsistent. Deep sleep is a stage, and staging is where they were inconsistent. So I'm not going to argue with the number, and I'm not going to build on it either.

[16]
I start with a sorting tool. I wrote a book about it, and you don't need the book for the next few minutes. Page 7 says this of the three systems it uses: "They are not brain regions or neural pathways. They are a sorting tool, a way to ask three questions when everything feels wrong at once."

[17]
Page 9 turns the three systems into three questions. Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Three questions. Not one of them asks for eight.

[18]
The first question is Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." For her the answer is a week, and we count it. You're going to do it with us, now, on your phone or your paper. Seven nights. For each one, two numbers, said out loud: the hours you got, and the hours you'd have wanted. A zero is an answer. A surplus is an answer.

[19]
Last night. Hours you got. Hours you'd have wanted. *(three seconds)* The night before. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)* Three nights ago. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)* Four nights ago. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)* Five nights ago. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)* Six nights ago. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)* Seven nights ago. Got. Wanted. *(three seconds)*

[20]
Now add up what you got, and take away what you wanted. That's your week, in hours. *(four seconds)* Mine's on this paper. *(The host reads her own difference, whatever it is on the day, in one short sentence.)* Hers is seven and a half hours short from Monday to Friday, three over at the weekend, four and a half short for the week. The weekend put back three of the seven and a half. Not the week. Part of it.

[21]
Here's why the count means more than the feeling, and this part is a finding. In Philadelphia the debt added up every night and the sleepiness barely rose after the first. What follows is my proposal, not a finding. Treat the number you've just written as the reading, and the feeling as the thing the reading stands in for. She felt fine on six. Her count says four and a half short, and feeling fine on it is what Philadelphia would predict.

[22]
The second question is Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." For her it's the watch. She checks it before she knows how she feels, and the number decides for her. And the thought, "I'm a bad sleeper", which arrives whenever she's tired. The score has become the cue. What I ask next is mine, not a finding: I ask her to leave the watch off for the week of the count. Naming the cue isn't a cure. It's a sort.

[23]
The third question is Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Observed: six hours on weeknights, a long lie-in, tiredness by Thursday. Assumed: that she's failing a rule, that a watch can see her sleep, and that the weekend settles the week. Needs action: two things I check before anything else, because they're conditions, not debts.

[24]
If she lies awake night after night, for months, that may have a name: insomnia disorder. Riemann and colleagues, in the European guideline of 2023, set out how it's diagnosed: by clinical interview, questionnaires and a sleep diary, not by a tracker. And the treatment that comes first is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, in person or digital. A sleep diary is the count you've just done, kept for longer.

[25]
If she snores loudly, if someone has seen her stop breathing in the night, if she wakes unrefreshed on hours that ought to be enough, that's a different question again. Obstructive sleep apnoea. Sleep medicine diagnoses it, with a test. I don't, a wrist doesn't, and I refer. Those three signs are a referral, not a debt.

[26]
So here's the better answer, and it isn't eight. It's a floor of seven for adults up to sixty-four, from the panel that voted. The rest is mine. Above that floor, a count instead of a rule. A week she can see written down, a weekend she's allowed to use and not allowed to trust to settle the week, and a watch in the drawer while she learns what her count says. A referral if the signs belong to a condition. A sorted week. Not a score.
