MOVEMENT ONE 

[1]
Patients tell me their cortisol is through the roof. They've read it somewhere, or they've measured it with a kit. The claim goes like this: cortisol is the stress hormone, and if you're stressed for long enough it either floods you with it or it wears your glands out. This episode asks one question about that. What is actually known.

[2]
Here's the verdict, and I'll say it again at the end, word for word. The evidence is weaker than the claim. A cortisol level says little about a person's stress. What is solid underneath is the shape of the day, and the slow second wave after alarm.

[3]
My name is Ivana Budišin. I'm a clinical psychologist in Luxembourg. In each episode I take one claim about the body and the mind, the kind patients bring into my room, and I separate what's solid from what's been stretched and from what's for sale. You'll see the sources on the screen and you'll hear me say them aloud. When a sentence is mine rather than a finding, I'll tell you, and there'll be nothing on the screen under it.

[4]
This is how the claim arrives in my room. "My cortisol is through the roof, that's why I can't sleep and I've put weight on round the middle." "I did a saliva test from a kit and my morning cortisol was low, so my adrenals are burnt out." "I've started taking ashwagandha to bring my cortisol down." "If you're stressed for years your adrenals just give up, and that's exactly how I feel." Four sentences. Four people. If you've said one of them, this is for you.

[5]
I take the claim seriously, because part of it is true. There is a hormone called cortisol. It does rise under stress. You can measure it in saliva. And there are two real diseases, one of too much and one of too little, and I'll name them both. So when someone arrives with a kit result, they've gone looking for a cause they could measure. The trouble isn't the looking. It's what a single number can carry, and what it can't.

 MOVEMENT TWO 

[6]
Let's start with what cortisol actually is. Ulrich-Lai and Herman set out the chain in 2009. Cortisol is the main human glucocorticoid. The adrenal cortex releases it at the end of a line that runs from the hypothalamus to the pituitary to the adrenal gland, and it feeds back on the first two. Kuo and colleagues, in 2015, set out its metabolic job, which is to keep glucose available to the brain. It drives the liver to make glucose and it opposes insulin in muscle and fat.

[7]
Cortisol is not a level. Lightman and Conway-Campbell set this out in 2010. Secretion runs on a daily rhythm, high in the morning and low at night, and riding on that rhythm is a pulse roughly every hour. Receptors in the tissues track each pulse. So the pattern carries information that a level doesn't. Then there's the morning. Pruessner and colleagues measured the first thirty minutes after waking, in 1997, and free cortisol rose by roughly fifty to seventy-five percent. A morning sample sits on a steep slope.

[8]
And the hormone doesn't have one direction. Sapolsky, Romero and Munck, in 2000, sorted its actions into four kinds: permissive, stimulatory, suppressive and preparative. Which kind you see depends on which endpoint you measure. Cain and Cidlowski, in 2017, described its immune job as regulatory. It shapes inflammation in both directions. So when you hear "cortisol is bad", or for that matter "cortisol is good", you're hearing a general statement the physiology doesn't make.

[9]
Now the clock, and this is the part that matters. There's a 2004 meta-analysis by Dickerson and Kemeny that pooled two hundred and eight laboratory studies of acute stressors. The autonomic response comes in seconds. Cortisol is the slower second wave. They found it peaks in saliva twenty-one to forty minutes after the stressor begins, and that the tasks that move it most combine uncontrollability with social evaluation. So whatever you feel in the first seconds of alarm, it isn't cortisol.

[10]
Chronic stress does not reliably raise cortisol. Miller, Chen and Zhou meta-analysed the human studies in 2007, and they found both raised and lowered activity of the axis, depending on when the stressor began, what kind it was, and who the person was. Output was highest near the onset of a stressor and declined as it went on. Traumatic or uncontrollable stressors went with a high, flat daily profile. And people with post-traumatic stress disorder showed lower output. Lower, not higher.

[11]
Low cortisol has a literature of its own. Fries and colleagues, in 2005, described low cortisol in chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia and post-traumatic stress disorder, and they proposed that it follows a long period of overactivity in the axis. So now you have two popular sentences. "Chronic stress equals high cortisol." "Adrenal fatigue equals low cortisol." Each one is half of a picture, and the evidence doesn't split the picture that way.

[12]
The shape of the day carries more than any point on it. Adam and colleagues pooled eighty studies in 2017. A flatter daily slope went with poorer health. It's a small effect, a correlation of point one four seven, and it's largest for immune outcomes. Here's the thing, though. That's a population association, not a test for one person. And nobody has shown that changing a person's slope changes an outcome.

[13]
A single measurement tells you very little. One sample depends on the time since you woke, the time since the last pulse, what you've eaten, and the previous forty minutes. Hellhammer, Wüst and Kudielka, in 2009, described salivary cortisol as an indirect index of the axis, one that may not track blood cortisol linearly while the axis is responding. In 2016 Stalder and colleagues set the consensus for measuring just the awakening response: several timed samples, on more than one day, with the waking time verified. Not one.

[14]
Hair tells a different story from saliva. Hair cortisol integrates months, and in 2017 Stalder and colleagues pooled sixty-six studies of it. They found it higher under ongoing chronic stress, and unrelated to how stressed people said they felt. Hold onto that. The feeling is what the person in my room arrives with.

[15]
Now the commercial edge, starting with adrenal fatigue. The idea is that chronic stress exhausts your adrenals. Cadegiani and Kater reviewed fifty-eight studies in 2016 and found no substantiation that it's a medical condition. They also noted weak assessment of fatigue, and cortisol methods that endocrinologists don't endorse.

[16]
The Endocrine Society has taken a position on this. Endocrine News reported it in 2017: the Society's position is that adrenal fatigue is not a medical condition. Its then president, Lynnette Nieman, added something I want you to hear. The symptoms are real, and they deserve a search for a genuine disorder. And in 2018, as Endocrine Today reported, the Society warned that the supplements sold for it are unregulated, and that some contain glandular extracts.

[17]
"Cortisol belly" borrows a real association and overstates it. Epel and colleagues, in 2000, found that women with a high waist-to-hip ratio secreted more cortisol under laboratory stress and didn't habituate. That was a cross-sectional design. Incollingo Rodriguez and colleagues reviewed the field in 2015 and found abdominal fat associated with a more responsive axis in many studies, but not all. Some showed blunted responses. As for "cortisol face", the round face it borrows is a sign of Cushing's syndrome. Not of a hard week.

[18]
Then there are the kits. I looked for a peer-reviewed validation of a consumer cortisol kit against clinical testing. I didn't find one. The best evidence for home saliva sampling is Debono and colleagues, in 2023, and they tested waking salivary cortisone, not cortisol, in people already at high risk of adrenal insufficiency. That's a screening tool for a defined disease in a referred population. Its numbers don't transfer to a healthy buyer. And a single morning value sits on every problem I've just described.

[19]
And then the supplements. Ashwagandha is the one with trials. Bachour and colleagues, in 2025, pooled fifteen trials and eight hundred and seventy-three adults, and reported lower cortisol against placebo, with low certainty and high heterogeneity. Whether that effect is real, and whether it matters, is open. The trials are small and they disagree with one another. And how stressed you feel doesn't reliably move with the hormone.

[20]
Two real conditions need naming, because the symptoms are real. Cushing's syndrome is sustained cortisol excess. Nieman and colleagues, in 2008, set out how an endocrinologist diagnoses it: late-night salivary cortisol, urine cortisol or dexamethasone suppression, repeated and read together. Addison's disease is the adrenals failing. Bornstein and colleagues, in 2016, described it as rare, dangerous and treatable, and confirmed by a short corticotropin test. If you have suggestive symptoms, you should be tested. Not told you have adrenal fatigue.

 MOVEMENT FOUR 

[22]
What I do instead doesn't start with the hormone. It starts with a sorting tool. I wrote a book about it, and you don't need the book for what follows. Here is what page 7 says about the three systems it uses: "They are not brain regions or neural pathways. They are a sorting tool, a way to ask three questions when everything feels wrong at once." A sorting tool isn't a claim about physiology, which is why it can follow a verdict like this one.

[23]
Page 9 turns the three systems into three questions. Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Three questions. Not one of them is about a hormone.

[24]
The person I'm going to describe is a composite. She isn't one patient. She's several, with the details changed. She's in her forties. She arrives with a printout from a kit: one saliva sample, taken at six in the morning, read as low. The kit's website has told her that her adrenals are exhausted. She sleeps badly. She's gained weight at the waist. She's been taking ashwagandha for two months. And she believes the claim completely.

[25]
I don't argue with the number. It's one sample on a curve that rises by half to three quarters in the thirty minutes after waking, from a waking she didn't record. What I do first is ask about the symptoms that belong to the two real diseases. If anything there is suggestive, she goes to an endocrinologist, who orders the tests I named. Because the symptoms are real. Nieman said so, and so do I.

[26]
The first question is Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." I ask it about the morning of the test. Four hours of sleep. Two coffees before the sample. An argument the night before. The hour itself, six, with no record of when she woke. Notice what the question does. It moves her from a hormone she can't see to a day she can describe. None of it is a diagnosis.

[27]
The second question is Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." For her, the thing that keeps pulling her back is the number. She checks the printout. She reads about it at night, which is exactly when she can't sleep. The thought is "my glands have given up", and it arrives every time she's tired, which is often, because she sleeps four hours. The number has become the cue. Naming that isn't a cure. It's a sort.

[28]
The third question is Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Observed: tiredness, a changed waist, waking at three. Assumed: that her glands are failing. Needs action: the observed things, with a doctor, and with the tests I named if the symptoms warrant them. The assumption doesn't need action. It needs a source. And it doesn't have one.

[29]
Then I ask her to do what page 7 asks of its reader. "Check your jaw. Check your breath. Check your shoulders." "What you found is a reading." What follows is my proposal, not a finding. The reading in her jaw is in the room now, and the hormone is twenty to forty minutes behind it. What she can use is what she can observe, and that's the day. That's the better answer. Not a lower number. A sorted morning, and a doctor for the symptoms.
