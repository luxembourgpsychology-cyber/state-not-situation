# Episode 1. "Cortisol is the stress hormone." Research notes

Researched 17 October 2026 against `series/BRIEF.md`. Every source was confirmed by web search this session; publisher pages could not be fetched, so where a finding rests on an abstract or a citing paper the reference list says so.

## 1. The claim as patients bring it

"My cortisol is through the roof, that's why I can't sleep and I've put weight on round the middle." "I did a saliva test from a kit and my morning cortisol was low, so my adrenals are burnt out." "I've started taking ashwagandha to bring my cortisol down." "If you're stressed for years your adrenals just give up, and that's exactly how I feel."

## 2. The kernel of good physiology

Cortisol is the main human glucocorticoid, released by the adrenal cortex at the end of the hypothalamic, pituitary, adrenal chain and feeding back on both. The autonomic and endocrine stress systems share circuits in limbic forebrain, hypothalamus and brainstem, and the balance between them shifts with the stressor (Ulrich-Lai & Herman, 2009).

It is not a level. Secretion runs on a circadian rhythm, high in the morning and low at night, and riding on it is an ultradian pulse roughly every hour. The pulsing is functional: receptor binding and gene transcription in tissues track each pulse, so the pattern carries information a level does not (Lightman & Conway-Campbell, 2010).

In the thirty minutes after waking, free cortisol rises by roughly fifty to seventy-five percent: the cortisol awakening response (Pruessner et al., 1997), with measurement rules set by international consensus (Stalder et al., 2016).

Its metabolic job is to keep glucose available to the brain, driving gluconeogenesis in the liver and reducing glucose uptake in muscle and fat by opposing insulin (Kuo et al., 2015). Its immune job is regulatory, shaping inflammation in both directions (Cain & Cidlowski, 2017). Sapolsky, Romero and Munck (2000) sorted its actions into permissive, stimulatory, suppressive and preparative, the category depending on the endpoint, which is why "cortisol is bad" and "cortisol is good" are both wrong as general statements.

In an acute stressor the autonomic response comes in seconds; cortisol is the slower second wave, and across 208 laboratory studies the tasks that move it most combine uncontrollability with social evaluation (Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004).

## 3. The overextension

Chronic stress does not reliably raise cortisol. Miller, Chen and Zhou (2007) meta-analysed the human literature and found both raised and lowered HPA activity, depending on the stressor's timing and type and on the person. Output is highest near onset and declines as the stressor persists; traumatic or uncontrollable stressors go with a high, flat daily profile; people with PTSD show lower output. Low cortisol is described in chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia and PTSD and proposed to follow a long period of HPA overactivity, possibly as a protective shift (Fries et al., 2005). "Chronic stress equals high cortisol" and "adrenal fatigue equals low cortisol" are each half of a picture the evidence does not split that way.

The shape of the day carries more than any point on it. Across 80 studies, flatter diurnal slopes went with poorer health, a small effect (r = .147) largest for immune outcomes (Adam et al., 2017). It is a population association, not an individual test.

A single measurement means very little: with hourly pulses on a steep daily curve, one sample depends on time since waking, time since the last pulse, food, and the previous forty minutes. Salivary cortisol is an indirect index of the axis and may not track plasma ACTH and cortisol linearly under response conditions (Hellhammer et al., 2009). The awakening response alone needs several timed samples on more than one day, with waking time verified (Stalder et al., 2016).

Hair cortisol integrates months. A meta-analysis of 66 studies found it higher under ongoing chronic stress but unrelated to perceived stress, so it tracks exposure, not how stressed a person feels (Stalder et al., 2017).

And the lag: salivary cortisol peaks twenty-one to forty minutes after a stressor starts (Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004). Whatever a person feels in the first seconds of alarm is not cortisol.

## 4. The commercial edge

Adrenal fatigue. The idea is that chronic stress exhausts the adrenals. Cadegiani and Kater (2016) reviewed 58 studies and found no substantiation that it is a medical condition, noting weak assessment of fatigue and cortisol methods not endorsed by endocrinologists. The Endocrine Society says it is not a real medical condition; its then president Lynnette Nieman added that the symptoms are real and deserve a search for a genuine disorder, and the Society warned that supplements sold for it are unregulated and some contain glandular extracts (Seaborg, 2017; Schaffer, 2018).

"Cortisol belly" and "cortisol face". Central fat and cortisol are linked, but not as claimed. Women with high waist-to-hip ratio secreted more cortisol under laboratory stress and did not habituate, in a cross-sectional design (Epel et al., 2000). A systematic review found abdominal fat associated with a more responsive axis in many but not all studies, some showing blunted responses (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2015). The facial fat the phrase borrows is a sign of Cushing's syndrome, not of a hard week.

Direct-to-consumer cortisol tests. No peer-reviewed validation of a consumer cortisol kit against clinical testing was found. The best evidence for home saliva sampling is a prospective study of waking salivary cortisone, not cortisol, in people already at high risk of adrenal insufficiency (Debono et al., 2023). That is a screening tool for a defined disease in a referred population; its predictive values do not transfer to a healthy buyer, and a single morning number sits on every problem in section 3.

Cortisol-lowering supplements. Ashwagandha has the trials. A meta-analysis of 15 trials and 873 adults reports lower cortisol against placebo, with low certainty and high heterogeneity (Bachour et al., 2025).

## 5. Real conditions that must be named

Cushing's syndrome is sustained cortisol excess, usually from a pituitary or adrenal tumour or prescribed steroids, diagnosed by an endocrinologist with specific tests such as late-night salivary cortisol, urine cortisol or dexamethasone suppression, repeated and read together (Nieman et al., 2008).

Addison's disease, primary adrenal insufficiency, is the adrenals failing. It is rare, dangerous and treatable, confirmed by a short corticotropin test, and anyone with suggestive symptoms should be tested rather than told they have adrenal fatigue (Bornstein et al., 2016).

## 6. The verdict

The evidence is weaker than the claim: cortisol is a pulsing, rhythmic hormone whose level at any moment says little about a person's stress, and exhausted adrenals, cortisol belly and a number to be lowered by a supplement are not supported; what is solid underneath is that cortisol is the slow second wave after alarm, arriving in tens of minutes and keeping glucose available, and that the pattern across a day, not the level, is where the health associations live.

## 7. What is genuinely open

Whether a flattened daily slope causes poor health, follows it, or shares a cause with it. No one has shown that changing a person's slope changes an outcome.

What hypocortisolism in chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia and some PTSD is: a protective adaptation, a marker of something else, or an artefact of sampling and timing.

Whether ashwagandha's cortisol effect is real and matters; the trials are small and heterogeneous, and perceived stress does not reliably move with the hormone.
