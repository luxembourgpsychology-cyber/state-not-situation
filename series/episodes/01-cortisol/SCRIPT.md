**1. Patients tell me their cortisol is through the roof.** They have read it, or measured it with a kit. The claim is that cortisol is the stress hormone, and that stress kept up long enough either floods a person with it or wears the glands out. This episode asks one question. What is actually known.

**2. Here is the verdict, and I will say it again later, word for word.** The evidence is weaker than the claim: a cortisol level says little about a person's stress. What is solid underneath is the shape of the day, and the slow second wave after alarm.

**3. My name is Ivana Budišin. I am a clinical psychologist in Luxembourg.** In each episode I take one claim about the body and the mind that patients arrive with, and I separate what is solid from what has been stretched and from what is for sale. The sources are on the screen and I say them aloud. Where a sentence is mine rather than a finding, I will say so, and nothing will be on the screen under it.

*Name band during the first two sentences.*

**4. This is how the claim arrives in my room.** "My cortisol is through the roof, that's why I can't sleep and I've put weight on round the middle." "I did a saliva test from a kit and my morning cortisol was low, so my adrenals are burnt out." "I've started taking ashwagandha to bring my cortisol down." "If you're stressed for years your adrenals just give up, and that's exactly how I feel." Four sentences. Four people.

*Claim card for the first quotation, at least six seconds.*

**5. I take the claim seriously, because part of it is true.** There is a hormone called cortisol. It rises under stress. It can be measured in saliva. There are two real diseases, of too much and of too little, and I will name them. A person who arrives with a kit result has looked for a cause they could measure. The trouble is not that they looked. It is what a single number can carry, and what it cannot.

## Movement two. The evidence. About 2:20 to 9:30

*Every sourced sentence has a band. A band enters on the first spoken word of the sentence it supports and holds for the whole claim, never under four seconds.*

**6. Start with what cortisol is.** Ulrich-Lai and Herman, in 2009, set out the chain: cortisol is the main human glucocorticoid, released by the adrenal cortex at the end of a line that runs from the hypothalamus to the pituitary to the adrenal gland, and feeding back on the first two. [Ulrich-Lai & Herman, 2009] Kuo and colleagues, in 2015, set out its metabolic job: to keep glucose available to the brain, by driving the liver to make glucose and by opposing insulin in muscle and fat. [Kuo et al., 2015]

**7. It is not a level.** Lightman and Conway-Campbell, in 2010, set out that secretion runs on a daily rhythm, high in the morning and low at night, with a pulse riding on it roughly every hour, and that receptors in the tissues track each pulse, so the pattern carries information a level does not. [Lightman & Conway-Campbell, 2010] Pruessner and colleagues, in 1997, measured the first thirty minutes after waking: free cortisol rises by roughly fifty to seventy-five percent. [Pruessner et al., 1997] A morning sample is a sample taken on a steep slope.

**8. The hormone does not have one direction.** Sapolsky, Romero and Munck, in 2000, sorted its actions into four kinds, permissive, stimulatory, suppressive and preparative, the kind depending on which endpoint is measured. [Sapolsky et al., 2000] Cain and Cidlowski, in 2017, described its immune job as regulatory, shaping inflammation in both directions. [Cain & Cidlowski, 2017] So "cortisol is bad" and "cortisol is good" are each a general statement the physiology does not make.

**9. Now the clock.** Dickerson and Kemeny, in 2004, pooled two hundred and eight laboratory studies of acute stressors. The autonomic response comes in seconds; cortisol is the slower second wave, and they found that it peaks in saliva twenty-one to forty minutes after the stressor begins, and that the tasks that move it most combine uncontrollability with social evaluation. [Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004] Whatever a person feels in the first seconds of alarm, it is not cortisol.

**10. Chronic stress does not reliably raise cortisol.** Miller, Chen and Zhou, in 2007, meta-analysed the human studies and found both raised and lowered activity of the axis, depending on when the stressor began, what kind it was, and on the person. [Miller et al., 2007] They found output highest near the onset of a stressor and declining as it persisted, traumatic or uncontrollable stressors going with a high, flat daily profile, and people with post-traumatic stress disorder showing lower output. [Miller et al., 2007]

**11. Low cortisol has its own literature.** Fries and colleagues, in 2005, described low cortisol in chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia and post-traumatic stress disorder, and proposed that it follows a long period of overactivity in the axis. [Fries et al., 2005] "Chronic stress equals high cortisol." "Adrenal fatigue equals low cortisol." Each is half of a picture, and the evidence does not split the picture that way.

**12. The shape of the day carries more than any point on it.** Adam and colleagues, in 2017, pooled eighty studies and found that a flatter daily slope went with poorer health, a small effect, a correlation of point one four seven, largest for immune outcomes. [Adam et al., 2017] That is a population association, not a test for one person. Nobody has shown that changing a person's slope changes an outcome.

**13. A single measurement means very little.** One sample depends on the time since waking, the time since the last pulse, food, and the previous forty minutes. Hellhammer, Wüst and Kudielka, in 2009, described salivary cortisol as an indirect index of the axis that may not track blood cortisol linearly while the axis is responding. [Hellhammer et al., 2009] Stalder and colleagues, in 2016, set the consensus for measuring the awakening response alone: several timed samples, on more than one day, with the waking time verified. [Stalder et al., 2016]

**14. Hair tells a different story from saliva.** Stalder and colleagues, in 2017, pooled sixty-six studies of hair cortisol, which integrates months, and found it higher under ongoing chronic stress but unrelated to how stressed people said they felt. [Stalder et al., 2017] The feeling is what the person in my room arrives with.

**15. Now the commercial edge, and first, adrenal fatigue.** The idea is that chronic stress exhausts the adrenals. Cadegiani and Kater, in 2016, reviewed fifty-eight studies and found no substantiation that it is a medical condition, noting weak assessment of fatigue and cortisol methods that endocrinologists do not endorse. [Cadegiani & Kater, 2016]

**16. The Endocrine Society has taken a position.** As Endocrine News reported in 2017, the Society's position is that adrenal fatigue is not a medical condition; its then president, Lynnette Nieman, added that the symptoms are real and deserve a search for a genuine disorder. [Seaborg, 2017] The Society has also warned, as Endocrine Today reported in 2018, that supplements sold for it are unregulated and that some contain glandular extracts. [Schaffer, 2018]

**17. "Cortisol belly" borrows a real association and overstates it.** Epel and colleagues, in 2000, found that women with a high waist-to-hip ratio secreted more cortisol under laboratory stress and did not habituate, in a cross-sectional design. [Epel et al., 2000] Incollingo Rodriguez and colleagues, in 2015, found abdominal fat associated with a more responsive axis in many studies but not all, some showing blunted responses. [Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2015] The round face that "cortisol face" borrows is a sign of Cushing's syndrome, not of a hard week.

**18. The kits.** I looked for a peer-reviewed validation of a consumer cortisol kit against clinical testing. I did not find one. The best evidence for home saliva sampling is Debono and colleagues, in 2023, who tested waking salivary cortisone, not cortisol, in people already at high risk of adrenal insufficiency. [Debono et al., 2023] That is a screening tool for a defined disease in a referred population. Its numbers do not transfer to a healthy buyer, and a single morning value sits on every problem I have just described.

**19. The supplements.** Ashwagandha has the trials. Bachour and colleagues, in 2025, pooled fifteen trials and eight hundred and seventy-three adults and reported lower cortisol against placebo, with low certainty and high heterogeneity. [Bachour et al., 2025] Whether that effect is real, and whether it matters, is open. The trials are small and they disagree with one another. And how stressed a person feels does not reliably move with the hormone.

**20. Two real conditions must be named, because the symptoms are real.** Cushing's syndrome is sustained cortisol excess, and Nieman and colleagues, in 2008, set out how an endocrinologist diagnoses it: late-night salivary cortisol, urine cortisol or dexamethasone suppression, repeated and read together. [Nieman et al., 2008] Addison's disease is the adrenals failing, and Bornstein and colleagues, in 2016, described it as rare, dangerous and treatable, confirmed by a short corticotropin test. [Bornstein et al., 2016] Anyone with suggestive symptoms should be tested, not told they have adrenal fatigue.

## Movement four. The application. About 9:50 to 14:00

**22. What I do instead does not start with the hormone.** It starts with a sorting tool. I wrote a book about it, and you do not need the book for what follows. The book's page 7 says this about the three systems it uses: "They are not brain regions or neural pathways. They are a sorting tool, a way to ask three questions when everything feels wrong at once." A sorting tool is not a claim about physiology.

**23. Page 9 turns the three systems into three questions.** Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Three questions. Nothing in them is a hormone.

*Three questions card for the whole of section 23; it leaves as section 24 begins.*

**24. The person I will describe is a composite.** She is not one patient. She is several, with the details changed. She is in her forties. She arrives with a printout from a kit: one saliva sample, taken at six in the morning, read as low. The kit's website has told her that her adrenals are exhausted. She sleeps badly. She has gained weight at the waist. She has been taking ashwagandha for two months. She believes the claim completely.

**25. I do not argue with the number.** It is one sample on a curve that rises by half to three quarters in the thirty minutes after waking, from a waking she did not record. What I do first is ask about the symptoms that belong to the two real diseases. If anything there is suggestive, she goes to an endocrinologist, who orders the tests I named. The symptoms are real. Nieman said so, and so do I. [Seaborg, 2017]

**26. The first question is Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour."** I ask it about the morning of the test. Four hours of sleep. Two coffees before the sample. An argument the night before. The hour itself, six, with no record of when she woke. The question moves her from a hormone she cannot see to a day she can describe. None of it is a diagnosis.

**27. The second question is Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task."** For her, the thing that keeps pulling her back is the number. She checks the printout. She reads about it at night, which is when she cannot sleep. The thought is "my glands have given up", and it arrives every time she is tired, which is often, because she sleeps four hours. The number has become the cue. Naming that is not a cure. It is a sort.

**28. The third question is Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?"** Observed: tiredness, a changed waist, waking at three. Assumed: that the glands are failing. Needs action: the observed things, with a doctor, and with the tests I named if the symptoms warrant them. The assumption does not need action. It needs a source, and it does not have one.

**29. Then I ask her to do what the book's page 7 asks of its reader.** "Check your jaw. Check your breath. Check your shoulders." "What you found is a reading." What follows is my proposal, not a finding. The reading in her jaw is in the room now, and the hormone is twenty to forty minutes behind it. [proposed] What she can use is what she can observe, and that is the day. [proposed] That is the better answer. Not a lower number. A sorted morning, and a doctor for the symptoms.
