## Part two. What the trend gets right. 0:10 to 0:43

**2. The right thing first: looks do change how people treat you.** Langlois and colleagues, in 2000: across eleven meta-analyses, attractive children and adults were judged more positively, and treated more positively. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82]

**3. So if you've noticed the world is kinder to good-looking people, you haven't been radicalised.** You've been paying attention. Collins made mogging its word of the year this month, and says younger people use it in a playful and ironic way. [Collins, 2026, 79] Fine by me. I'm here for the rating scale under it.

## Part three. The word. 0:43 to 1:53

**4. The word is "objective".** Hand a ruler to anyone and your desk stays the same width. The scale's supposed to work like that: one face, one score, whoever's holding it. So do two people rating the same face get the same answer?

*The artefact card's rule returns alone for this section: the line, the ten ticks, no
numbers, the one red tick. Nothing is placed on it.*

**5. In October 2015, Germine, Wilmer and their colleagues published an answer in Current Biology.** They'd run a face test on a website, TestMyBrain. And they had twins: five hundred and forty-seven identical pairs, two hundred and fourteen non-identical pairs, two hundred faces. [Germine et al., 2015, 58]

*D1 begins here: two identical line figures, seen from behind as raters, each holding a
short unnumbered strip. It holds its end frame through section 7.*

**6. One measurement.** About half of the variation in how people rated those faces came from taste people share. About half came from the individual rater's own taste. And identical twins weren't significantly more alike in their taste than non-identical twins. [Germine et al., 2015, 58]

*In D1, each figure makes one tick on its strip. The two ticks land in different places.
The gap between them is the only red on the frame.*

**7. Now the honest part, because Langlois and Germine sound as if they disagree.** Langlois found that raters agree about who's attractive: a crowd's average is steady. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82] Germine found that, rater by rater, about half the variation is personal. [Germine et al., 2015, 58] Both hold. A score can describe a crowd. You don't date a crowd.

*D1's end frame: a faint row of many strips behind the two figures, their ticks clustering,
and the two front ticks still apart.*

## Part four. Who likes whom. 1:53 to 2:35

**8. So what does predict who likes whom?** In 2017 Joel, Eastwick and Finkel tried, with speed-dating. Before anyone met, each person reported more than a hundred traits and preferences. Then four-minute dates, and a machine-learning model given all of it. [Joel, Eastwick and Finkel, 2017, 81]

*D3 begins here: two rows of line figures at small tables, all alike, none ranked.*

**9. The model could predict part of who tends to be desired.** It could predict none of who would desire whom in particular. [Joel, Eastwick and Finkel, 2017, 81] More than a hundred facts each, and the machine learned what every wedding guest already knows. Nobody saw those two coming.

*In D3, grey threads run from each figure toward the row opposite. One red thread joins
two figures no grey thread had joined. End frame.*

**10. None of that cancels Langlois.** Looks still get you treated better. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82] But nothing those people reported in advance said who'd want whom. [Joel, Eastwick and Finkel, 2017, 81]

## Part five. The testosterone turn. 2:35 to 4:22

**11. Now T-maxxing, and who's selling the test.** Gram and colleagues, 2026, studied forty-six high-reach "low T" posts on Instagram and TikTok, and found testosterone sold to young, fit men as a matter of masculinity. [Gram et al., 2026, 60] The University of Sydney's release adds that none of the posts cited scientific evidence. [University of Sydney, 2026, 61]

**12. Here's what the selling looks like.** In 2022 Dubin and colleagues sent a secret shopper to seven online testosterone platforms in the United States. His script: thirty-four, low energy, low libido, wants children one day, and testosterone that's normal. Six of the seven offered him testosterone. [Dubin et al., 2022, 62] He said "normal". They heard "customer".

*D4 begins here: the line figure holding one sheet, the word "normal" on it in mono, in
front of seven doors in a row. Six doors open. One stays shut. The word "normal" is the
only red. End frame holds through section 13.*

**13. And the cost, for a man who wants children.** A World Health Organization trial, published in 1990, gave weekly testosterone injections to two hundred and seventy-one healthy men. Within six months, sixty-five percent had no sperm in their semen. [World Health Organization Task Force, 1990, 65] It was a contraceptive trial, on a fixed dose, so the figure stays there. But the hormone sold as masculinity was being tested as birth control.

**14. None of this means low testosterone isn't real.** The condition is hypogonadism, and an endocrinologist diagnoses it. The Endocrine Society's guideline, 2018, recommends against routine screening of men in the general population. A diagnosis needs symptoms and signs, with consistently low testosterone, measured fasting, in the morning, and repeated. [Bhasin et al., 2018, 66]

**15. The morning isn't a detail.** Brambilla and colleagues, 2009: in men aged thirty to forty, testosterone at four in the afternoon was twenty to twenty-five percent lower than at eight in the morning. [Brambilla et al., 2009, 63] Nothing happened to them. It got later. So: a doctor, and a morning blood test. Twice. Not a link in a bio.

*D2 begins on "Nothing happened to them": the figure's day as one ink line falling from a
mono "08:00" to a mono "16:00". The drop between the two is the only red. It leaves on
"So: a doctor".*

## What the editor changed, in full

Narrowed, ten. Section 2: "pooled eleven meta-analyses" became "across eleven
meta-analyses", because the authors ran eleven and did not pool other people's; "than
unattractive ones" was dropped, so nobody in the room is sorted into a group. Section 3:
"it's used" became "younger people use it", which is whom Collins credits. Section 4: "do
two people using it" became "two people rating the same face", because the twin study
used ordinary ratings and never this scale. Section 5: "the answer" became "an answer".
Section 7: "the scale can describe a crowd" became "a score can describe a crowd", and
Germine is named for its half. Section 10: "No number says who walks through it with you"
became "nothing those people reported in advance said who'd want whom", which is what the
study tested. Section 11: "the researchers report" became "the University of Sydney's
release adds", which is where the fact-check found the sentence. Section 13: the trial is
dated. Section 16: "comparing yourself with other men" became "comparing your looks with
other people's", the manual's sense. Section 21: "You found out what the check was
answering" became "a first guess".

Cut, eight factual sentences. "This scale is supposed to be objective" and "That's a twin
study from 2015, and the source is on your screen", from the opening. "The twin paper's
title: mostly environments, not genes", which section 6 already says. The American
Association of Orthodontists on mewing. "So the most reliable way to get a low result is
a late appointment." "At first sight, looks count", which no checked source says: the
review found the effect even among people who know each other. "Looks open the door", for

tested that scale, and the line he carries out says what the evidence does.

The funny lines, tested aloud for a calm voice. Survived unchanged: "you haven't been

Cut: the late appointment. Every one is aimed at an instrument, a business or a

No stretch now runs past twenty seconds without a number, a turn or a line. The slowest

injections stopped, the estimated median time to recovery was 3.7 months. It is not on
