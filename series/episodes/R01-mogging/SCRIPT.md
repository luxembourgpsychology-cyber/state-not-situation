# R01. Mogging, looksmaxxing and T-maxxing. The horizontal. Script

Written 10 November 2026 against `FACTCHECK.md` of the same day, which governs every fact
here, and against `series/WHY-IT-WORKS.md`, `VIRAL.md`, `series/BRIEF.md` and
`series/FORMAT.md`. A working draft until the author has read it. Nothing factual in it
comes from anywhere but the fact-check's safe list and its confirmed entries; where the
fact-checker narrowed a fact, it is spoken as narrowed; where a fact was marked
unconfirmed, it is not spoken. The book is quoted once, in its printed words, from page 9.

How to read this file. Ten parts, cut into numbered sections of at most seventy spoken
words. The first sentence of each section is in bold and is fixed: it is the sentence to
memorise, and the sentence the editor finds in `CUES.md`. The rest of a section may move
in the mouth, provided no fact changes and no source is dropped. A sourced sentence names
its source aloud and carries the citation in square brackets after it, with its
`CITATIONS.md` number; the brackets are for the cue sheet and are not spoken. A sentence
marked `[proposed]` is the author's own reading or proposal, is announced as such aloud,
and has nothing on screen under it. Stage notes are in italics and are not spoken. The
parts are headed here for the editor and are never named on camera.

Who it is spoken to. The young man inside the trend, as an equal, in the second person.
Every joke in it is aimed at an instrument, a clinic or a study design. None is aimed at
him. The test from `VIRAL.md` holds for every line: would he be embarrassed if his sister
sent it to him? If yes, cut it.

Twelve sources are spoken: Germine 2015, Langlois 2000, Collins 2026, the American
Association of Orthodontists 2024, Brambilla 2009, Joel, Eastwick and Finkel 2017, Gram
2026 with the University of Sydney's release, Dubin 2022, the World Health Organization
Task Force 1990, Bhasin 2018, the American Psychiatric Association 2022 and Harrison 2016.
The drawn pieces are D1 to D4 and are specified by the motion designer; the notes here say
only what each must show. They are drawn on the series' line figure, which has no face
and no name. Nothing drawn in this episode has features, a score or a ranked pair.

Where this departs from `WHY-IT-WORKS.md`, section 6. The commitment beat ("finish this
sentence") is not in this cut; the brief for this episode asked for ten parts and a close
that says what would change her mind, and the retraction line in section 24 carries that
job alone. The composite is not used either: the person in the room is the viewer.

At 150 words a minute: 1,143 spoken words, about 7:35 of speech; with the artefact card
and five seconds of scripted silence in section 22, about 7:45 before the end card. The
brief's ceiling is 1,150 words. If it must come down, cut section 8's first claim and
section 12's last two sentences before anything with a source under it.

[Germine et al., 2015, 58] I'm a clinical psychologist. The trend is right about one thing, and it's wrong about one word. Here's the word.

## Part two. What the trend gets right. About 0:25 to 1:00

**2. First, what it gets right, and I won't be grudging about it.** Looks change how people treat you. Langlois and colleagues, in 2000, pooled eleven meta-analyses. Attractive children and adults were judged more positively, and treated more positively, than unattractive ones. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82]

**3. So if you've noticed the world is kinder to good-looking people, you haven't been radicalised.** You've been paying attention. Collins made mogging its word of the year this month, and says it's used in a playful and ironic way. [Collins, 2026, 79] Fine by me. I'm here for the scale under it.

## Part three. The word. About 1:00 to 2:10

**4. The word is "objective".** Hand a ruler to anyone and your desk stays the same width. The scale under mogging is supposed to work that way: one face, one score, whoever's holding it. So, do two people using it get the same answer?

*The artefact card's rule returns alone for this section: the line, the ten ticks, no
numbers, the one red tick. Nothing is placed on it.*

**5. In October 2015, Germine, Wilmer and their colleagues published the answer in Current Biology.** They'd run a face test on a website, TestMyBrain. And they had twins. Five hundred and forty-seven identical pairs, two hundred and fourteen non-identical pairs, two hundred faces. [Germine et al., 2015, 58]

*D1 begins here: two identical line figures, seen from behind as raters, each holding a
short unnumbered strip. It holds its end frame through section 7.*

**6. One measurement.** About half of the variation in how people rated those faces came from taste people share. About half came from the individual rater's own taste. And identical twins weren't significantly more alike in their taste than non-identical twins. [Germine et al., 2015, 58]

*In D1, each figure makes one tick on its strip. The two ticks land in different places.
The gap between them is the only red on the frame.*

**7. Now the honest part, because Langlois and Germine sound as if they disagree.** Langlois found that raters agree about who's attractive: a crowd's average is steady. Rater by rater, about half the variation is personal. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82] [Germine et al., 2015, 58] So the scale can describe a crowd. And you don't date an average.

*D1's end frame: a faint row of many strips behind the two figures, their ticks clustering,
and the two front ticks still apart.*

## Part four. Three claims on the measuring tape. About 2:10 to 2:50

**8. Three claims that went on the measuring tape, fast.** One: one scale fits everyone. The twin paper's title: mostly environments, not genes. [Germine et al., 2015, 58] Two: mewing reshapes your jaw. The American Association of Orthodontists, 2024: no scientific evidence that it reshapes the jawline. [American Association of Orthodontists, 2024, 70]

**9. Three: one blood test gives you your testosterone.** Brambilla and colleagues, 2009: in men aged thirty to forty, testosterone at four in the afternoon was twenty to twenty-five percent lower than at eight in the morning. [Brambilla et al., 2009, 63] Nothing happened to them. It got later. So the most reliable way to get a low result is a late appointment.

*D2 begins on "Nothing happened to them": the figure's day as one ink line falling from a
mono "08:00" to a mono "16:00". The drop between the two is the only red. It holds to the
end of the section.*

## Part five. Who likes whom. About 2:50 to 3:35

**10. So if a score can't settle it, what predicts who likes whom?** In 2017 Joel, Eastwick and Finkel tried, with speed-dating. Before anyone met, each person reported more than a hundred traits and preferences. Then four-minute dates, and a machine-learning model given all of it. [Joel, Eastwick and Finkel, 2017, 81]

*D3 begins here: two rows of line figures at small tables, all alike, none ranked.*

**11. The model could predict part of who tends to be desired.** It could predict none of who would desire whom in particular. [Joel, Eastwick and Finkel, 2017, 81] More than a hundred facts each, and the machine learned what every wedding guest already knows. Nobody saw those two coming.

*In D3, grey threads run from each figure toward the row opposite. One red thread joins
two figures no grey thread had joined. End frame.*

**12. Here's the honest counterweight.** At first sight, looks count. Langlois again: raters agree about who's attractive, and treat them better. [Langlois et al., 2000, 82] Looks open the door. No number says who walks through it with you.

## Part six. The testosterone turn. About 3:35 to 5:10

**13. Now T-maxxing, and who's selling the test.** Gram and colleagues, 2026, studied forty-six high-reach "low T" posts on Instagram and TikTok, and found testosterone sold to young, fit men as a matter of masculinity. The researchers report that none of the posts cited scientific evidence. [Gram et al., 2026, 60] [University of Sydney, 2026, 61]

**14. Here's what the selling looks like, as a story.** In 2022 Dubin and colleagues sent a secret shopper to seven online testosterone platforms in the United States. His script: thirty-four, low energy, low libido, wants children one day, and testosterone that's normal. Six of the seven offered him testosterone. [Dubin et al., 2022, 62] He said "normal". They heard "customer".

*D4 begins here: the line figure holding one sheet, the word "normal" on it in mono, in
front of seven doors in a row. Six doors open. One stays shut. The word "normal" is the
only red. End frame holds through section 15.*

**15. And the cost, for a man who wants children.** A World Health Organization trial gave weekly testosterone injections to two hundred and seventy-one healthy men. Within six months, sixty-five percent had no sperm in their semen. [World Health Organization Task Force, 1990, 65] It was a contraceptive trial, on a fixed dose, so the figure stays there. But the hormone sold as masculinity was being tested as birth control.

**16. None of this means low testosterone isn't real.** The condition is hypogonadism, and an endocrinologist diagnoses it. The Endocrine Society's guideline, 2018, recommends against routine screening of men in the general population. A diagnosis needs symptoms and signs, with consistently low testosterone, measured fasting, in the morning, and repeated. [Bhasin et al., 2018, 66] So: a doctor, and a morning blood test. Twice. Not a link in a bio.

## Part seven. The mirror. About 5:10 to 6:10

**17. Back to the face, carefully.** Checking the mirror. Comparing yourself with other men. Asking to be told you look fine. The diagnostic manual, DSM-5-TR, lists behaviours like those among the repetitive behaviours and mental acts of body dysmorphic disorder. [American Psychiatric Association, 2022, 71] Rating isn't there by name. Calling it a check with a number attached is my judgement. [proposed]

*Host only. No mirror, no reflection, no drawn piece. The band carries the manual's name
and year; the criterion is paraphrased and is never set on screen in quotation marks.*

**18. Doing those things is not the same as having the disorder.** One feature alone isn't a diagnosis, and I can't diagnose anyone through a lens. [American Psychiatric Association, 2022, 71] My reading: the trend is daily practice in the behaviours that diagnosis is built from. [proposed]

**19. If the checking has taken over your days, that's worth an assessment, and the disorder is treatable.** Harrison and colleagues, 2016: across seven randomised trials, cognitive behavioural therapy did better than waiting or a placebo therapy. [Harrison et al., 2016, 77] Plainly: if thoughts about your appearance have become distressing, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, speak to a doctor or a crisis line in your country.

*The safety sentence is said at the same pace as everything else, to the lens, with nothing
on screen but the host; the Harrison band has left before it begins. No statistic about
suicide is spoken or shown anywhere in the episode.*

## Part ten. The close. About 7:15 to 7:45

**23. So: looks matter, and a number that depends on who's looking isn't a fact about your face.** The references are in the description, in full, and the transcript's on the site.

**24. And here's what would change my mind.** Show me a study where strangers rate the same faces and the individual rater explains almost none of the variation, where raters agree the way rulers agree. Until then, the word's a joke. Keep it. The scale isn't objective. Hand it back.

## For the author: decisions this draft took

The counterweight in section 12 rests on Langlois alone. The research file's stronger
sentence, that attractiveness is the main driver of speed-dating choices, comes from a
paper the fact-check did not reach, so it is not spoken.

The orthodontist behind mewing is not named, and the regulator's and the High Court's
decisions are left out. They are on the safe list, but naming him would break the rule
against naming people, and the association's sentence carries the point alone.

"Rating" is not among the manual's examples as the fact-check saw them. Section 17 says
so and marks the link as her judgement.

The afternoon figure in section 9 is said with its age range, thirty to forty, and is not
moved onto a younger man. Nobody in that study was under thirty.

The first names of the twin study's authors and the city they worked in were not
confirmed, so the story's place is the website the test ran on, which was.

"This month" in section 3 holds for any publication date in November 2026. After that it
becomes "on the third of November".

Section 23's first sentence is the vertical's closing line, word for word. It is the line
the viewer is meant to carry out, so it is said the same way in both.
