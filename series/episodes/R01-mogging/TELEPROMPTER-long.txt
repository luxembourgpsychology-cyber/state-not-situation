[01]
Half the variation in face ratings comes from the rater. I'm a clinical psychologist. The trend's right about one thing, wrong about one word.

[02]
The right thing first: looks do change how people treat you. Langlois and colleagues, in 2000: across eleven meta-analyses, attractive children and adults were judged more positively, and treated more positively.

[03]
So if you've noticed the world is kinder to good-looking people, you haven't been radicalised. You've been paying attention. Collins made mogging its word of the year this month, and says younger people use it in a playful and ironic way. Fine by me. I'm here for the rating scale under it.

[04]
The word is "objective". Hand a ruler to anyone and your desk stays the same width. The scale's supposed to work like that: one face, one score, whoever's holding it. So do two people rating the same face get the same answer?

[05]
In October 2015, Germine, Wilmer and their colleagues published an answer in Current Biology. They'd run a face test on a website, TestMyBrain. And they had twins: five hundred and forty-seven identical pairs, two hundred and fourteen non-identical pairs, two hundred faces.

[06]
One measurement. About half of the variation in how people rated those faces came from taste people share. About half came from the individual rater's own taste. And identical twins weren't significantly more alike in their taste than non-identical twins.

[07]
Now the honest part, because Langlois and Germine sound as if they disagree. Langlois found that raters agree about who's attractive: a crowd's average is steady. Germine found that, rater by rater, about half the variation is personal. Both hold. A score can describe a crowd. You don't date a crowd.

[08]
So what does predict who likes whom? In 2017 Joel, Eastwick and Finkel tried, with speed-dating. Before anyone met, each person reported more than a hundred traits and preferences. Then four-minute dates, and a machine-learning model given all of it.

[09]
The model could predict part of who tends to be desired. It could predict none of who would desire whom in particular. More than a hundred facts each, and the machine learned what every wedding guest already knows. Nobody saw those two coming.

[10]
None of that cancels Langlois. Looks still get you treated better. But nothing those people reported in advance said who'd want whom.

[11]
Now T-maxxing, and who's selling the test. Gram and colleagues, 2026, studied forty-six high-reach "low T" posts on Instagram and TikTok, and found testosterone sold to young, fit men as a matter of masculinity. The University of Sydney's release adds that none of the posts cited scientific evidence.

[12]
Here's what the selling looks like. In 2022 Dubin and colleagues sent a secret shopper to seven online testosterone platforms in the United States. His script: thirty-four, low energy, low libido, wants children one day, and testosterone that's normal. Six of the seven offered him testosterone. He said "normal". They heard "customer".

[13]
And the cost, for a man who wants children. A World Health Organization trial, published in 1990, gave weekly testosterone injections to two hundred and seventy-one healthy men. Within six months, sixty-five percent had no sperm in their semen. It was a contraceptive trial, on a fixed dose, so the figure stays there. But the hormone sold as masculinity was being tested as birth control.

[14]
None of this means low testosterone isn't real. The condition is hypogonadism, and an endocrinologist diagnoses it. The Endocrine Society's guideline, 2018, recommends against routine screening of men in the general population. A diagnosis needs symptoms and signs, with consistently low testosterone, measured fasting, in the morning, and repeated.

[15]
The morning isn't a detail. Brambilla and colleagues, 2009: in men aged thirty to forty, testosterone at four in the afternoon was twenty to twenty-five percent lower than at eight in the morning. Nothing happened to them. It got later. So: a doctor, and a morning blood test. Twice. Not a link in a bio.

[16]
Back to the face, carefully. Checking the mirror. Comparing your looks with other people's. Asking to be told you look fine. The diagnostic manual, DSM-5-TR, lists behaviours like those among the repetitive behaviours and mental acts of body dysmorphic disorder. Rating isn't there by name. Calling it a check with a number attached is my judgement.

[17]
Doing those things is not the same as having the disorder. One feature alone isn't a diagnosis, and I can't diagnose anyone through a lens. My reading: the trend is daily practice in the behaviours that diagnosis is built from.

[18]
If the checking has taken over your days, that's worth an assessment, and the disorder is treatable. Harrison and colleagues, 2016: across seven randomised trials, cognitive behavioural therapy did better than waiting or a placebo therapy. Plainly: if thoughts about your appearance have become distressing, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, speak to a doctor or a crisis line in your country.

[19]
This next part is my clinical reading, not a finding. I think the trend gives you three things. A rulebook: school explained photosynthesis and left attraction to you. Something to do tonight. And a rank. Keep the first two. The rank is the only one that needs somebody else to lose.

[20]
Here's something to do instead, in under thirty seconds. I wrote a book, and you don't need it for this. Page 9 prints a question under the word Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." Think of the last time you checked. A mirror, a front camera, a rating thread.

[21]
Now pick one of the four. A message. A thought. A cue. An unfinished task.

One word, and you're done. It's a first guess at what the check was answering. That's my suggestion, not a finding.

[22]
Here's what would change my mind. Show me a study where strangers rate the same faces and the individual rater explains almost none of the variation, where raters agree the way rulers agree. The references are in the description, in full, and the transcript's on the site.

[23]
The word's a joke, so keep it. The scale's the part to hand back. Looks matter. A number that depends on who's looking isn't a fact about your face.
