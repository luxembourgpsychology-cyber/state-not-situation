# R01 Mogging: the viral brief

For the author, from the content team. Written 10 November 2026 from web searches run
that day. It builds on `series/WHY-IT-WORKS.md` (the mechanism and the ten-second
template), `series/TRENDS-2026-11.md` (item 6) and `series/BRIEF.md` (the constraints).

How to read the facts here. Direct page fetches were not used; every fact is taken from
search result summaries, so each one must be re-read at its URL before it goes on screen
or into `CITATIONS.md`. Where a search came back empty, that is said. Nothing is from
memory. Lines offered for the script are proposals in the team's words, not hers and not
the book's; the book is quoted only where `BRIEF.md` already quotes it.

One finding changes the whole brief, so it comes first. Collins did not crown a symptom.
It crowned a joke. The dictionary describes "mogging" as used mostly "in a playful and
ironic way" and lists forms like "carmogging" and "lifemogging" (Euronews, 3 November
2026). A video that treats every boy who says "mogged" as a patient will be wrong, and he
will know it in one second. The video has to hold both: the word is mostly a joke, and
the scale underneath the joke is not.

## 2. The emotional engine

What a man of fifteen to twenty-five gets from this content is three things, in this
order.

A rulebook. Attraction is the least explained subject of his adolescence. Here it has
named parts, angles and a number. The vocabulary sounds like anatomy, and anatomy sounds
like truth.

Agency. Softmaxxing gives him a list he can start tonight. The Hopkins piece concedes
this part is lower-risk, and the video must concede it too.

A rank. He learns where he stands. This is the part that hurts and the part that holds
him, because a rank can be checked again tomorrow. Halpin's team named the cost
"masculine demoralisation" (Sociology of Health & Illness, 2025).

A fourth thing rides on top: the joke. Saying "mogged" about a car is funny, and the
irony lets him use the scale without admitting he believes it.

What a parent, sister or girlfriend feels on meeting the vocabulary is exclusion first,
then alarm. She cannot tell the joke from the belief. The words that reach her are the
frightening ones. Her instinct is to correct, which is the one move that confirms she is
outside.

Which audience the hook should address. Him. The people around him are the distribution;
he is the viewer who decides in three seconds. `WHY-IT-WORKS.md` section 5(c) is the
reason: a second-person state gets shared, a second-person instruction casts the viewer
as the one being corrected. A hook written for a worried mother is an instruction to him
by the time it arrives. A hook written for him, in his words, conceding the true part
first, is something she can forward without it reading as an accusation.

So the short speaks to him and is safe to be sent. The long video's title and
description carry the dictionary peg, which is what the senders are searching this week.
The test for every line: would he be embarrassed if his sister sent it to him? If yes,
cut it.

## 3. Fifteen hooks for the first three seconds

Each is under fifteen words. Each names the fact it rests on. None is verified beyond a
search summary. "Type" follows the brief: reversal, definition, number, clinician's
observation, reply to the artefact.

1. "This scale is supposed to be objective. About half the rating is the rater." Reply to
   the artefact. Rests on: Know Your Meme's account of PSL as an "objective" framework;
   Germine et al. 2015, twin study, about 48% of variation in face ratings explained by
   shared preferences (https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/822593; journal and
   exact figure to confirm in the paper); Hönekopp 2006, private and shared taste roughly
   equal (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p5793).

2. "Looks do change how people treat you. That part of looksmaxxing is true." Reversal.
   Rests on: Langlois et al., Psychological Bulletin 126, 2000, eleven meta-analyses,
   attractive people judged and treated more positively
   (https://core.ac.uk/download/191855612.pdf). Effect sizes not seen.

3. "The dictionary calls mogging outshining. The diagnostic manual calls repeated
   comparing a criterion." Definition. Rests on: Collins, 3 November 2026; DSM-5 body
   dysmorphic disorder criterion B, repetitive behaviours or mental acts including
   comparing one's appearance with that of others. The criterion wording came only from
   a third-party template
   (https://carepatron.com/templates/body-dysmorphic-disorder-dsm-5-criteria). Check it
   against DSM-5-TR itself. One criterion is not a diagnosis, and the next sentence must
   say so.

4. "Forty-six testosterone posts were studied. None of them cited any evidence." Number.
   Rests on: Gram et al., Social Science & Medicine; University of Sydney, 3 February
   2026.

5. "Researchers read the rating threads. In nearly every one, someone was insulted."
   Number. Rests on: Halpin et al. 2025. Secondary sources say "every" and "nearly
   every"; the paper's full text decides
   (https://lifestance.com/blog/looksmaxxing/; CBC, https://www.cbc.ca/1.7499752).

6. "Mogging began around 2016 in bodybuilding. Last week it became word of the year."
   Reversal. Rests on: Collins via CP24 and Euronews. "Last week" is true only if
   published by 16 November.

7. "Collins counted mogging up 210 percent this year. Many people still can't define it."
   Number. Rests on: Collins; LBC.

8. "PSL is usually read as three forums. One had hate in its name."
   Definition. Rests on: Know Your Meme. Two of the three names contain "hate"; "one" is
   safe, and the alternative expansions must be acknowledged in the long video.

9. "Softmaxxing is the low-risk part. Doctors say so. The trouble starts at max."
   Reversal. Rests on: Johns Hopkins, August 2026; Dictionary.com, "short for
   maximizing".

10. "An hour a day on your looks is where clinicians start asking questions." Number.
    Rests on: BDD Foundation screening card, first question
    (https://bddfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/GP-Card-A5-Document.pdf).

11. "About one man in two hundred has clinically low testosterone. Estimates vary."
    Number. Rests on: UNSW and The Conversation, August 2025. Older men and men with
    diabetes or obesity carry most of it.

12. "Dictionary.com chose maxxing. Collins chose mogging. One is effort. One is a
    comparison." Definition. Rests on: both announcements; Euronews's distinction.

13. "Collins says mogging is mostly a joke. The scale under it isn't one."
    Reversal. Rests on: Collins, "playful and ironic"; the second sentence is her
    judgement and needs the PSL sources behind it on screen.

14. "Nobody tells me they were mogged. They tell me they can't stop checking."
    Clinician's observation. Rests on: her own practice. Use only if it is true of the
    people she sees, and say it as a general observation, never as a case.

15. "Young men who compared themselves to the videos felt worse. The others did not."
    Number. Rests on: Flinders, Body Image, 29 March 2026. "The others did not" goes
    beyond the summary we saw; read the paper or cut the second sentence.

## 4. The artefact

No real face, no minor, no creator. Three proposals, ranked.

First: the word as a dictionary entry. Set by us in the series serif on cream, ink rule
under it: "mogging, noun, slang" and the gloss "outshining someone or something", with
the mono band "Collins Word of the Year, 3 November 2026". It is the news peg, it is a
word (which `WHY-IT-WORKS.md` says is what travels), and it is the one artefact that
shows no body at all. Do not reproduce Collins's page or logo; set the word ourselves and
cite it.

Second: an empty scale drawn by us. One ink line, ten ticks, no face anywhere, one red
mark sliding along it. This is the trend's own object and the best partner for hook 1.
It ranks second only because a scale is the visual grammar of rating content, and
YouTube limits repeated recommendation to teens of content that "compares physical
features and idealizes some types over others" (section 8). Keep it abstract and it
should read as commentary.

Third: a search bar typing "what does mogged mean". It is the senders' own gesture this
week and costs nothing to make. It is weaker for him: he already knows.

Rejected: the mirror. It invites a reflected face, and it is the checking behaviour
itself.

## 5. The one-word payoff

The template line is "the trend is right about one thing, and it's wrong about one
word". Five candidates, ranked.

First. Right: appearance changes how people are treated (Langlois 2000). Wrong word:
"objective". The scale was built to be objective (Know Your Meme), researchers call it
pseudoscientific (Solea and Sugiura, 2026), and about half of the variation in ratings
of a face sits with the rater (Germine 2015; Hönekopp 2006). This pair concedes the most
and corrects the least, which is the series' verdict form. It also leads straight to the
book's own distinction without borrowing its words: a number that depends on who is
looking is not a fact about the face.

Second. Right: effort on yourself works; sleep, skin, training and clothes are real
(Johns Hopkins). Wrong word: "max". A maximum has no stopping point, and the dictionary
chose that word this fortnight. Best for the long video's second story.

Third. Right: comparing yourself with others is ordinary. Wrong word: "mog". The word
turns a glance into a result with a winner. Weaker because Collins says the word is
mostly a joke, so he can fairly answer that he never meant it. The social comparison
literature was not searched today; it needs a source before use.

Fourth. Right: low testosterone is a real condition, in about one man in two hundred
(UNSW). Wrong word: "low". The posts moved it onto healthy young men (Gram et al.).
Strong, but it is the T-maxxing episode, not this one. Keep it for a follow-up short.

Fifth. Right: men went there for help and for each other. Wrong word: "help". This is
Halpin's title, so it must be credited, and it is the heaviest. Long video only.

## 6. What the viewer does

Under thirty seconds, on his own body or day, cannot be failed, no face is rated. Three
proposals.

The other jaw check. The trend's jaw check is done with the eyes. This one is done with
the jaw. Teeth apart, tongue down, let it hang for three breaths, and notice whether it
was clenched. Whatever he finds is the result. It uses the trend's own gesture, it is
visible on camera, and it moves attention from how the jaw looks to how it feels. She
does it with him in real time. No health claim is attached to it.

The last time, sorted. Think of the last time you felt mogged, or checked. Then the three
questions as `BRIEF.md` gives them from page 9: "What came before this moment. What keeps
pulling me back. What seems at stake." One breath each. There is no right answer, which
is why it cannot be failed. Check the wording against the printed page before it goes on
a card. This is the application beat and it is the strongest fit for the series.

The count. How many times today did you look, in a mirror or a front camera? Guess the
number. Only a number, no judgement. For the long video, the BDD Foundation's hour
question sits beside it as the line where a clinician would want to hear more. It is
third because a count can tip into self-diagnosis, the "signs you" trap named in
`WHY-IT-WORKS.md` section 4.

## 8. Risks

Mocking boys. The most likely failure, and it can happen in tone alone. A raised eyebrow
at "canthal tilt" is enough. Avoid by pronouncing every term correctly and flatly, by
conceding the true part before the correction, and by never using the vocabulary for a
laugh. The Romanian study in `WHY-IT-WORKS.md` says sarcasm earns engagement. It would
earn it here from the wrong audience.

Sounding like a parent. Words to keep out of the first twenty seconds: dangerous, toxic,
worried, harmful, should. So is any sentence that begins with "you need". She is a
clinician describing a measuring instrument, not an adult assessing a boy.

Pathologising a joke. Collins says the word is mostly playful and ironic. If the video
implies that saying "mogged" is a symptom, it is wrong on the facts. Say plainly that
most people who use the word are joking, and that the video is about the scale.

How-to detail. Never show or describe the method of bone smashing, any drug, peptide,
dose, diet or surgical step. Name the practice in one clause and move on. No tongue
posture demonstration either. The viewer action in section 6 was chosen so that nothing
on screen could be copied to harm.

Self-harm content. The forum study's hardest finding is that users were encouraged
toward suicide. If it is used at all, it belongs in the long video, in one sentence, with
no quotation of the comments, and with the referral line beside it. Leave it out of the
short.

Showing people. No real face, no minor, no named creator, no stitched clip. Coverage this
year names one streamer in its headlines; the episode does not.

Platform policy. Check both pages directly before upload; neither could be read today.

## 9. Posting plan

Length. The short at 50 to 60 seconds, as the template sets it; do not stretch it to
carry a second idea. The long at six to eight minutes, held there by cutting a story and
never a source. No platform publishes a length benchmark (`WHY-IT-WORKS.md`, section 4),
so the measure is her own "stayed to watch" share.

Sequence. Short first in the feed, as `WHY-IT-WORKS.md` section 6 sets out, with one
practical condition: the short ends on "the longer version is on the channel", so the
long video must already be live when the short goes out. Upload the long, then publish
the short the same hour.

Timing. Collins announced on 3 November. The peg is a week old today and the phrase "last
week" expires on 16 November. Publish inside that window if the fact-check allows; if it
does not, drop hook 6 and lose nothing else. No verified data on best day or hour was
found, and none should be assumed.

Three follow-up shorts, each cut from the long video, each with its own artefact and its
own word.

One, days three to four: T-maxxing. Artefact: the number 46 in mono. "Forty-six posts,
