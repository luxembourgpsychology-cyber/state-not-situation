# R01 Mogging: research brief on testosterone ("T-maxxing")

Compiled 10 November 2026 from web searches run that day. Direct page fetches were refused by the session's network (sydney.edu.au, eurekalert.org, pubmed, ku.dk), so every figure below comes from search-returned abstracts and summaries, not from full texts. Each reference carries its status. Nothing here is cited from memory.

## 1. The claims as they circulate

The trend's premise is a collapse. A 2026 headline ran "Men's testosterone levels have fallen by half in last 50 years, scientists warn" (Yahoo News UK, 2026), reporting an unreviewed conference abstract. Clinic videos repeat that today's men are "not the same as their fathers"; the exact posts were not retrievable, so quote them only after viewing (citation needed: the original videos).

The second claim is diagnostic. The lead author of the 2026 content analysis summarised it: "Young, healthy men are being told that common experiences like tiredness, stress or changes in libido are signs that something is medically wrong" (University of Sydney, 2026). One TikTok creator with about 102,000 followers told viewers that missing morning erections signals low testosterone and to get checked (University of Sydney, 2026).

The third is the protocol. One 2026 summary describes the feed as ten eggs a day, raw steak and unofficial supplements (Feeld, 2026); cold showers and sun exposure recur. A urologist quoted by Newsweek (2026) said social media "has turned testosterone into a proxy for masculinity and vitality".

## 2. What the hormone does across a day

Testosterone peaks in the morning. In men aged 30 to 40, levels at 16:00 were 20 to 25% lower than at 08:00; at 70 the gap was about 10% (Brambilla et al., 2009). An afternoon sample in a young man can therefore read "low" for no medical reason.

Sleep moves it. Ten healthy men of about 24, restricted to five hours a night for one week, had daytime testosterone 10 to 15% lower (Leproult & Van Cauter, 2011). A meta-analysis reports a clear fall after a full night without sleep (Su et al., 2021; pooled figures not confirmed). Levels also drop in acute illness, and UK guidance says not to test then (Jayasena et al., 2022; wording seen only in a secondary summary).

It varies between days. Two measurements in the same man differ by more than 18 to 28% about half the time, depending on the hormone, and the authors concluded one sample is generally not sufficient (Brambilla et al., 2007).

This is why the Endocrine Society requires symptoms and signs plus "unequivocally and consistently low" levels, measured fasting in the morning and repeated (Bhasin et al., 2018). The European Academy of Andrology asks for symptoms plus "repeatedly low morning fasting" total testosterone (Corona et al., 2020). The European Association of Urology specifies fasting samples between 7 and 11 am (Salonia et al., 2025).

## 3. The generational decline

Travison et al. (2007) followed Boston-area men aged 45 to 79 across three waves from 1987 to 2004 (1,374, 906 and 489 men) and found age-matched testosterone about 1.2% lower per calendar year, a fall that press accounts say persisted after adjusting for obesity, smoking and medication. It is one regional cohort of middle-aged and older men, not young men.

Later work divides. In Danish surveys the decline was no longer significant after adjusting for body mass index (Andersson et al., 2007). A United States survey comparison of 1988 to 1991 with 1999 to 2004 found no evidence of decline (Nyante et al., 2012). In men aged 15 to 39, mean total testosterone fell from 605 to 451 ng/dL between 1999 and 2016, but three different assays were used and the authors list this as a limitation (Lokeshwar et al., 2021). A systematic review of 1,256 papers found a negative trend that held after adjusting for age, body mass index and assay (Santi et al., 2025).

So a modest population decline is plausible, obesity explains part of it, and assay change clouds the size. What remains open is the cause, the true rate, and whether it means anything for one young man. The harmonised lower limit for healthy non-obese men aged 19 to 39 is about 264 ng/dL (Travison et al., 2017); a population average drifting down is not a diagnosis.

## 4. Screening

The Endocrine Society states: "We recommend against routine screening of men in the general population for hypogonadism" (Bhasin et al., 2018). The European Association of Urology advises screening only men with symptoms (Salonia et al., 2025).

Gram et al. (2026) analysed 46 high-reach posts, 30 on Instagram and 16 on TikTok, from accounts followed by more than 6.8 million people. Four themes emerged: low testosterone as a crisis of masculinity and sexual performance; its rebranding from an older man's problem to a young, fit man's problem; self-optimisation toward stereotyped masculine ideals; and a binary of "real man" against feminine. Per the university's release, 85% of posts came from individuals rather than health organisations, 67% carried direct purchase links, 72% had financial interests (owning a brand selling tests, selling boosters or therapy, or sponsorship), and none cited scientific evidence (University of Sydney, 2026). Private clinics and direct-to-consumer products were framed as faster than standard care. The article appeared online on 26 December 2025; February 2026 is the press release.

## 5. Clinics and exogenous testosterone

A secret shopper posing as a 34-year-old with low energy, normal testosterone and a wish for future fertility approached seven United States platforms. Six offered testosterone; five of those six did not discuss polycythaemia (Dubin et al., 2022).

Exogenous testosterone switches off the body's own axis. Weekly injections made 65% of 271 healthy men azoospermic within six months, with a median 3.7 months to recovery after stopping (World Health Organization Task Force, 1990). The Endocrine Society recommends against treatment in men planning fertility soon (Bhasin et al., 2018). Men whose haematocrit reached 52% on therapy had more cardiovascular and clotting events in the first year, 5.15% against 3.87% (Ory et al., 2022).

## 6. Steroids and SARMs

Pooled lifetime anabolic steroid use is 3.3% globally and 6.4% in males (Sagoe et al., 2014). Among United States school students in 2024 lifetime use was 1.4% or lower in every grade (Miech et al., 2024). In 2,256 young Canadians, 25.7% of men scored at clinical risk for muscle dysmorphia (Ganson et al., 2023); the steroid association is reported in coverage, not the abstract.

The FDA warned in April 2023 that SARMs are unapproved, promoted to teenagers on social media, and linked to liver injury and risk of heart attack and stroke (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2023). No national prevalence figure for SARMs was found. A UK Anti-Doping panel survey reported 33% of 16 to 25 year olds had bought them (UK Anti-Doping, 2026); treat this online-panel figure cautiously.

## 7. What moves it, and what does not

Sleep, as above. Weight loss in men with obesity: a low-calorie diet raised total testosterone by 2.87 and bariatric surgery by 8.73 (Corona et al., 2013; nmol/L presumed, units not confirmed). Resistance training raises it briefly after a session; resting levels do not change (pooled effect 0.00; Potter et al., 2021).

Ashwagandha: a 2026 meta-analysis reports 57 ng/dL more than placebo in men (citation needed: authors, Planta Medica, 92(8), 790 to 805), about a tenth of a young man's median, from small short trials. Tongkat ali: pooled standardised effect 1.35 from five small trials, strongest in men already low (Leisegang et al., 2022). Zinc: benefit where deficient; only 8 of 38 reviewed studies were clinical (Te et al., 2023). Vitamin D: no effect in 98 healthy men (Lerchbaum et al., 2017). Boron: eight men, uncontrolled (Naghii et al., 2011); no good trial. Sunlight and cold: no good trial found. Of 50 marketed "boosters", 90% claimed to raise testosterone; 61.5% of their ingredients had no data at all (Clemesha et al., 2020).

## 8. What must be named and referred

Hypogonadism is real: symptoms with confirmed low levels, then LH and FSH to separate testicular from pituitary causes (Bhasin et al., 2018). Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) affects roughly 1 in 660 men, and only about a quarter are ever diagnosed (Bojesen et al., 2003; Gravholt et al., 2018). Pituitary disease presents as low testosterone with low or normal LH; the prolactin and imaging pathway was not confirmed this session (citation needed: Bhasin et al., 2018, section 1.3). The route is a GP's two morning samples, then an endocrinologist or andrologist. A psychologist names it and refers.
