# R01 Mogging: social psychology research

Compiled 10 November 2026 from web searches run that day. Direct fetches of papers were refused, so every figure below comes from an abstract, a repository record or press coverage, and is marked accordingly in the reference list. Nothing is cited from memory.

## 1. Where the words came from

AMOG, "alpha male of the group", is pickup-artist jargon of the early 2000s; Wiktionary cites Strauss's *The Game* (2005) for it (Merriam-Webster, n.d.; Wiktionary, n.d.). The verb "mog" is documented on 4chan's /fit/ board from 19 May 2016 (Know Your Meme, n.d.-a). "PSL" names three forums in sequence: PUAHate, SlutHate after the 2014 Isla Vista killings, and Lookism.net, registered June 2015, where a looksmaxxing board is archived from July 2015 (Know Your Meme, n.d.-b). "Black pill" is traced to a commenter called Paragon on the blog Dalrock on 24 November 2011, adopted three days later by the blog Omega Virgin Revolt (Know Your Meme, n.d.-c). These dates rest on a meme archive and dictionaries, not primary posts. The scholars who have documented the move to TikTok are Solea and Sugiura (2023), Halpin et al. (2025) and the linguist Aleksic (2025).

Collins named "mogging" Word of the Year on 3 November 2026, defined as outshining someone, with use up 210%, mostly "in a playful and ironic way", born of AMOG in bodybuilding culture and "briefly adopted by the manosphere" (CP24, 2026; LBC, 2026). Dictionary.com chose "maxxing", traced to "maximize" and gaming's "min-maxing"; the coverage found does not mention incel forums (KATV, 2026). Its date, 27 October, comes from the trends file and was not reconfirmed.

## 2. The claims against the literature

Objective to a decimal. Group averages agree well; Langlois et al. (2000) report raters agree within and across cultures. Individuals do not. Hönekopp (2006) found private taste about as powerful as shared taste. Germine et al. (2015) found two random people agree about 48% of the time, and identical twins' individual preferences correlated only 0.22. No validation study of the PSL scale was found.

Features. Rossetti et al. (2013) measured faces in three dimensions: ratios were unrelated to attractiveness and most were not golden. Holland (2008) found the Marquardt mask method faulty. Pallett et al. (2010) found the preferred spacings are those of an average face. For canthal tilt the one study located used retouched female faces (Bashour and Geist, 2007, citation needed for journal details). Nothing peer reviewed was found on "hunter eyes" or jaw angle: citation needed. On facial width-to-height ratio, a meta-analysis found a "small" link with aggression (Haselhuhn et al., 2015); in 137,163 people it predicted none of 55 scales (Kosinski, 2017), and nothing among 1,000 executives (Wang et al., 2019).

Life outcomes. Hamermesh and Biddle (1994) found a plainness penalty of 5 to 10 percent of earnings. Kanazawa and Still (2018) found the premium vanished once health, intelligence and personality were controlled. Wong and Penner (2016) found grooming explained all of the premium for women and half for men. The halo is real and uneven: strong for social competence, near nothing for integrity, about d = .49 overall by a secondary report (Dion et al., 1972; Eagly et al., 1991).

## 3. A photo against a meeting

Honesty first: in speed-dating, attractiveness is the main driver of choices (Asendorpf et al., 2011). But consensus is not the whole of it. Eastwick and Hunt (2014) found more relationship variance than target variance, and less consensus with acquaintance; exact percentages: citation needed. Joel et al. (2017) predicted 7 to 27% of who is generally desired and none of who desires whom. Hall and Compton (2017) had students rate ten photographs, talk to one person for ten minutes and rate again; only that person's rating moved, mostly from the middle and low range. Among 167 couples, matching on looks was strong when dating began within a month and much weaker after long acquaintance (Hunt et al., 2015). Expressiveness predicted liking independently of looks (Friedman et al., 1988).

## 4. Social comparison

Festinger (1954) proposed that people evaluate themselves by comparison with others when no objective yardstick exists. Across 156 studies comparison predicted body dissatisfaction (Myers and Crowther, 2009). In daily sampling of 150 women, upward comparisons on social media brought the worst mood (Fardouly et al., 2017). In men, experiments with muscular images give d = -0.22 (Barlett et al., 2008). Mogging means out-ranking, so every use is a comparison, and a scale with decimals guarantees someone above. Halpin et al. (2025) coded 8,072 comments; nearly every rating thread held an insult or unfavourable comparison (CBC, 2025).

## 5. What the trend is right about

Looks do matter at first sight, and grooming is changeable. Former incels told Maryn et al. (2024) the community said three things: it is not your fault, you belong here, you are special. O'Malley and Helm (2022) found membership raised self-esteem. Among 3,000 young men, 63% watch masculinity influencers; 43% find them motivating and 27% report feeling worthless (Fisher et al., 2025). These are adjacent samples; interviews with looksmaxxers are unpublished: citation needed.

## 6. Three stories, ranked

First. Laura Germine and Jeremy Wilmer, Boston and Wellesley, 2015. Measurement: 547 identical twin pairs rated 200 faces and their personal tastes correlated 0.22. Payoff: about half of what you find beautiful is yours alone, and even your identical twin does not share it.

Second. Samantha Joel, Paul Eastwick and Eli Finkel, 2017, speed-dating events of 2005 and 2007, 350 undergraduates (Northwestern University: place to confirm). Measurement: a hundred traits predicted none of the pair-specific desire. Payoff: you can predict who is popular, and not who will like whom.

Third. Adolf Zeising, Germany, 1854, proposed the golden section in the body; Fechner doubted it in 1876 (Cain, 2026); Rossetti measured real faces in 2013. Payoff: the ratio was an idea before it was a measurement, and it failed the measurement.

No speed-dating study in which photo ratings failed outright was found; Hall and Compton is the nearest.
