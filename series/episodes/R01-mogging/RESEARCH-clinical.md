# R01. Mogging and looksmaxxing. Clinical research notes

Direct fetches of PubMed, the AAD, the National Archives and publisher pages were refused by the network, so findings rest on abstracts, repository records and search snippets; section 8 says which. ## 1. Body dysmorphic disorder

In DSM-5-TR, body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) sits among the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders. Criterion A is preoccupation with perceived flaws in appearance that others cannot see or see as slight. Criterion B is repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response at some point in the course. Criterion C is clinically significant distress or impairment. Criterion D excludes weight and shape concerns better explained by an eating disorder (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022). The specifier "with muscle dysmorphia" applies when the person is preoccupied with the idea that his or her build is too small or insufficiently muscular (APA, 2022).

A systematic review gives a weighted prevalence of 1.9% in community adults, 2.2% in adolescents and 3.3% in students (Veale et al., 2016). A United States telephone survey found 2.4%: 2.5% of women and 2.2% of men (Koran et al., 2008). In England's national survey of 7,654 young people aged 5 to 19, point prevalence was 1.0%; 1.9% in adolescents against 0.1% in children, and 1.8% in girls against 0.3% in boys (Krebs et al., 2024). The adult sex ratio is near even; the youth figure is not, and whether boys are missed is an open question.

Mean age at onset was 16.7 years in two adult samples, and early onset went with more suicide attempts (Bjornsson et al., 2013).

A meta-analysis of 17 studies found BDD associated with suicidality, OR 3.63 (95% CI 2.62 to 4.63); 3.30 for attempts and 2.57 for ideation. The authors rate the studies few and of low quality, and deaths by suicide are scarcely studied (Angelakis et al., 2016). In 200 people with BDD, 78.0% reported lifetime suicidal ideation and 27.5% an attempt (Phillips et al., 2005).

## 2. Appearance media and boys

Experimental evidence predates social media. Two meta-analyses of 25 studies found media pressure toward the muscular ideal associated with men feeling worse about their bodies, d = -0.19 in correlational studies and d = -0.22 in experiments (Barlett et al., 2008). A review of 15 exposure experiments found a small significant effect, and suggested men already satisfied with their bodies were protected (Blond, 2008).

For social media, a meta-analysis of 63 samples and 36,552 people found a small association with body image disturbance, r = .169, not separated by sex in the abstract (Saiphoo & Vahedi, 2019). A three-wave study of 1,911 adolescents found largely no direct effect of use on body satisfaction; the indirect path ran through comparison, in boys and girls alike (Jarman et al., 2024). In 1,553 boys and men, how often they viewed muscular bodies, supplement and steroid content went with muscle dysmorphia symptoms; the design is cross-sectional (Ganson et al., 2025).

The honest limits: effects are small, most designs are correlational, and a boy already preoccupied may seek the content. No boy-specific pooled estimate for social media was found. Citation needed: a meta-analysis of social media experiments in males.

## 3. Research on looksmaxxing itself

The literature is thin. The one empirical health study found is a qualitative analysis of 8,072 comments from a looksmaxxing forum with six million monthly visitors. Its authors describe "masculine demoralisation": users are treated as failed men, pushed toward drastic body alteration and encouraged to self-harm (Halpin et al., 2025). It is sociology of forum text. It diagnosed no one.

A criminology study of incel accounts, videos and comments on TikTok argues that blackpill beliefs are normalised there through covert language (Solea & Sugiura, 2023). A commentary in a facial plastic surgery journal places looksmaxxing between self-enhancement and self-harm (Konig et al., 2025); its content was seen only through secondary summaries. An otolaryngology paper (Muntaner Vives et al., 2026) and a nursing column on bone smashing (Landers & Parsh, 2024) exist; only the second could be characterised.

No study was found that applied BDD criteria or a BDD screen to people who looksmaxx. The link to BDD is clinical opinion (Tovey, 2026), not a measured rate.

## 4. The practices

Mewing. A letter in the Journal of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery reported no research supporting tongue posture as an alternative to orthodontics or jaw surgery (Lee et al., 2019). The American Association of Orthodontists (2024) said the practice lacks scientific backing. The British Orthodontic Society (2024) said there is no scientific evidence that people can change the shape of their face by holding teeth and tongue in a closed position. The General Dental Council's Professional Conduct Committee erased Mike Mew from the Dentists' Register on 6 November 2024, and the High Court dismissed his appeal on 15 May 2026 (*Mew v General Dental Council*, 2026).

Bone smashing. A nursing column lists the risks as irreparable damage to soft tissue, airway and eyes (Landers & Parsh, 2024). A hospital statement says the videos misread Wolff's law and that repeated blows produce swelling and microfractures without improving appearance (Mater, 2026). No peer-reviewed case report was found. Press reporting describes a trollish origin and little evidence that anyone is doing it (The Week, n.d.). Call it mostly talk, dangerous if acted on; do not call it an epidemic.

Jaw exercisers. The only source found is a letter in the British Dental Journal comparing the load on the masseter to bruxism and disputing the maker's claims (Nagpal & Gupta, 2018). No trial of cosmetic benefit was found.

The PSL scale. It is a forum convention named for three forums, PUAHate, SlutHate and Lookism, with no scientific standing (Know Your Meme, n.d.). No validation study was found, and none is expected.

## 5. The behaviours and the criteria

Mirror checking and reassurance seeking are named examples of Criterion B behaviours. Comparing one's appearance with that of others is the named example of a Criterion B mental act (APA, 2022). Mogging is comparison by definition. Camouflaging is listed among BDD's repetitive behaviours in clinical descriptions; whether the criterion text names it was not confirmed. Rating one's own face is not named in DSM-5-TR. Mapping it to Criterion B, as checking or comparing with a number attached, is clinical judgment.

None of this is the disorder. Criterion B alone diagnoses nothing. BDD requires the preoccupation of Criterion A and the distress or impairment of Criterion C (APA, 2022). Most boys who compare do not have BDD. The trend teaches the behaviours; it does not hand out the diagnosis.

## 6. Treatment

NICE recommends cognitive behavioural therapy including exposure and response prevention for BDD: first line, family-involved and age-adapted for children and young people, with an SSRI added for 12 to 18 year olds with moderate to severe impairment who have not responded; for adults, CBT, an SSRI or both according to severity (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE], 2005).

Seven randomised trials (N = 299) showed CBT superior to waitlist, with gains held at two to four months (Harrison et al., 2016). Fifteen trials (N = 905) of psychological treatments gave g = -0.97 on BDD symptoms, larger against waitlist than against active controls (Liu et al., 2024). In the one placebo-controlled fluoxetine trial found, 53% responded against 18% (Phillips et al., 2002).

Cosmetic procedures rarely improve BDD symptoms, and most people with BDD do poorly after them (Bowyer et al., 2016). Pooled prevalence of BDD among plastic surgery patients was 18.6% (Kaleeny & Janis, 2024).

## 7. The seventeen-year-old who rates his face daily

Clinical judgment from here; the sources stop at section 6.

A clinician would not argue about the number. Telling him he looks fine is reassurance, and reassurance is one of the behaviours. She would ask what the sources make diagnostic: how many hours a day it takes, what he has stopped doing, whether he avoids school, friends or photographs, and whether he has thought about ending his life. That last question is asked directly, because of section 1.

If the answer is an hour or more a day with avoidance or distress, that warrants assessment, and the treatment in section 6 exists and works. If it is not, the better answer is still about the behaviour and not the face: the rating is a check, checks relieve for minutes and return, and the experiment is to drop it for a fortnight and see what happens to the worry. Before any procedure, an assessment comes first (Bowyer et al., 2016).
