# The season

Ten episodes, decided 20 October 2026. Three acts follow the book's three systems, Time,
Attention and Safety, without naming them until the finale. Every episode stands alone.
If a viewer can tell which act they are in before the finale, the book is showing too
early.

Each entry gives the slug the folder takes, the claim as it circulates, the hook the
episode opens on, the kernel of good science underneath, the source leads, and the drawing.
Source leads are leads, not verified sources: nothing here reaches a script until the
citation check has confirmed it. The hook is a draft the hook writer may beat.

The standard for a hook: a specific, true thing said in the first sentence, either a
scene from the consulting room or a fact that reverses what the viewer expects. Never a
question, never "everything you know is wrong", never a number the sources do not carry.
The verdict follows inside thirty seconds, as the format requires.

## Act one. Time

### 01 · cortisol · done

**Claim.** Cortisol is the stress hormone. Chronic stress floods you with it or wears your
adrenals out.
**Hook.** Patients tell me their cortisol is through the roof.
**Kernel.** Cortisol is a pulsing hormone on a daily rhythm; the pattern carries the
information, not the level; it peaks twenty-one to forty minutes after a stressor begins.
**Sources.** Verified, in `CITATIONS.md` 6, 7, 11 to 30.
**Drawing.** Done: M1 to M8.

### 02 · sleep · open

**Claim.** You need eight hours, and you can catch up at the weekend.
**Hook, draft.** The most influential sleep book of the decade has a chapter that its own
cited studies do not support, and a graduate student found it by reading the footnotes.
**Kernel.** Sleep pressure builds while awake and clears while asleep (the two-process
model). Restriction to six hours for two weeks produces deficits people stop noticing.
Sleep need varies between people more than the eight-hour rule allows. Weekend recovery
sleep partly restores performance and partly does not.
**Source leads, to verify.** Borbély (1982), the two-process model. Van Dongen, Maislin,
Mullington and Dinges (2003), cumulative restriction. Walker (2017), *Why We Sleep*, and
Guzey (2019), the critique of it, which is a blog essay and must be labelled as such.
Cappuccio, D'Elia, Strazzullo and Miller (2010), sleep duration and mortality
meta-analysis. Depner et al. (2019), weekend recovery sleep.
**Drawing.** The figure through a week: a pressure gauge in the head that rises each day
and never fully empties; the weekend lie-in drains it partway.
**Application.** The patient who arrives certain she is "a bad sleeper" because of one
rule, sorted by Time.

### 03 · hunger · open

**Claim.** Willpower runs on glucose. Being hangry is your blood sugar talking.
**Hook, draft.** In 2007 a famous experiment showed that a glass of lemonade with sugar
restored self-control and a glass with sweetener did not. In 2016, twenty-three
laboratories tried to find the underlying effect at once, and did not.
**Kernel.** The brain's glucose use does not rise measurably with effortful control. The
ego-depletion effect behind the glucose story failed a large multi-site replication.
Irritability when hungry is real and is partly about context and labelling, not glucose
alone. Hypoglycaemia in diabetes is a different and real thing.
**Source leads, to verify.** Gailliot et al. (2007), the lemonade study. Kurzban (2010),
does the brain consume additional glucose during self-control. Hagger et al. (2016), the
multi-lab replication of ego depletion. Bushman, DeWall, Pond and Hanus (2014), glucose
and aggression in couples. MacCormack and Lindquist (2019), feeling hangry as context and
attribution.
**Drawing.** The figure with a glucose line that barely moves while the "effort" label
rises; then the same figure with the day's meals marked.
**Application.** The patient who reads a hard afternoon as a verdict on her marriage,
sorted by Time: what came before this moment.

## Act two. Attention

### 04 · goldfish · open

**Claim.** Our attention span has dropped to eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish's.
**Hook, draft.** The eight-second figure appears in thousands of articles. Nobody has
found the study. The goldfish, for what it is worth, has a memory of months.
**Kernel.** The figure traces to a marketing report citing a statistics website citing
nothing. Attention has no single span; what research measures is task switching, sustained
attention under load, and the cost of interruption, which is real and quantified.
**Source leads, to verify.** Maybin (2017), BBC, the search for the source. Mark, Gudith
and Klocke (2008), the cost of interrupted work. Mark (2023), *Attention Span*, for the
measured decline in screen-switching intervals and what it does and does not mean.
Agranoff and Davis (1968) or a later review on goldfish memory, for the aside.
**Drawing.** No goldfish. The figure at a desk, a timeline of switches drawn as ticks,
each tick a cost.
**Application.** The patient who believes her mind is broken because she cannot read a
page, sorted by Attention: what keeps pulling me back.

### 05 · dopamine · open

**Claim.** You can detox from dopamine. Modern life has fried your reward system.
**Hook, draft.** Dopamine is not the pleasure chemical. The man who showed what it
actually does spent twenty years watching a monkey's neurons fire before the juice
arrived, not after.
**Kernel.** Dopamine signals prediction error and wanting, not liking. There is no
measurable "dopamine level" to lower by abstaining from phones. The practice that goes by
the name is stimulus control, which has an evidence base under its real name.
**Source leads, to verify.** Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997), reward prediction error.
Berridge and Robinson (1998, 2016), wanting versus liking. Sepah (2019), the essay that
coined "dopamine fasting" and later disowned the misreading. Lembke (2021), *Dopamine
Nation*, as the popular source, read fairly.
**Drawing.** The figure, a cue, and the dopamine mark firing at the cue, not at the
reward.
**Application.** The patient on a "detox" who is in fact avoiding one specific cue, sorted
by Attention.

## Act three. Safety

### 06 · memory · open

**Claim.** I remember exactly how it happened.
**Hook, draft.** The morning after the Challenger exploded, a class of students wrote down
where they were. Three years later, a quarter of them were wrong about every detail, and
rated their confidence as high as ever.
**Kernel.** Vivid memories of shocking events decay like ordinary memories while
confidence does not. Memory is reconstructed at each recall. This is uncontroversial and
it is the safest ground in the act.
**Source leads, to verify.** Neisser and Harsch (1992), the Challenger study. Talarico and
Rubin (2003), confidence and consistency in flashbulb memories. Hirst et al. (2015), the
ten-year 9/11 study. Loftus and Palmer (1974), the wording effect.
**Drawing.** The figure recalling: the same scene redrawn three times, each a little
different, the confidence mark unchanged.
**Application.** The patient certain of what was said in an argument, sorted by Safety:
what have I observed, what am I assuming.

### 07 · amygdala · open

**Claim.** The amygdala hijacks your brain. Fear bypasses thought.
**Hook, draft.** A woman known in the literature by her initials has no amygdala on
either side. She can be startled. She cannot be frightened by a snake. And she can still
be made to panic, in a way that surprised the people who tested her.
**Kernel.** The amygdala is a threat detector, not a fear generator; the "low road" story
is contested; fear as experience and threat response as physiology are separable. The
"hijack" is a 1995 book's phrase, not a finding.
**Source leads, to verify.** Goleman (1995), the origin of "hijack". LeDoux (1996, 2015),
the two-system account and its revision. Feinstein, Adolphs, Damasio and Tranel (2011),
patient S.M. Feinstein et al. (2013), the carbon dioxide panic result. Pessoa and Adolphs
(2010), from a low road to many roads.
**Drawing.** The figure's head with the almond-shaped mark, the threat line fast, the
"fear" word arriving later and elsewhere.
**Application.** The patient who says "it was my amygdala" as if that closed the matter,
sorted by Safety.

### 08 · vagus · open

**Claim.** Tone your vagus nerve. Polyvagal theory explains trauma, safety and connection.
**Hook, draft.** The vagus nerve is real, slow breathing does change your heart rhythm
within a minute, and the theory most often sold alongside both has been rejected by the
physiologists who study the nerve.
**Kernel.** Vagal influence on the heart and the breathing effect are measured. Polyvagal
theory's core claims about a "ventral" and "dorsal" vagus and their evolutionary story
are disputed on comparative physiology. Clinical usefulness and physiological truth are
different questions and must be kept apart on camera.
**Source leads, to verify.** Porges (1995, 2011), the theory. Grossman and Taylor (2007),
respiratory sinus arrhythmia and vagal tone. Grossman (2023), the critique in
*Biological Psychology*. Lehrer and Gevirtz (2014) and Vaschillo et al. (2002), already
verified in `CITATIONS.md`.
**Drawing.** The figure in profile, the nerve drawn from brainstem to heart and gut, the
breath slowing and the heart's swing widening.
**Application.** The patient doing vagus exercises for a problem that is a Safety
question, sorted as one.

### 09 · body-keeps · open

**Claim.** Trauma is stored in the body.
**Hook, draft.** The best-selling psychology book of the last decade says the body keeps
the score. Clinically, something true is being pointed at. Scientifically, the sentence
does not mean what readers think it means, and the difference matters for what treatment
they seek.
**Kernel.** Trauma changes physiology, sleep, startle and threat appraisal; that is
documented. Memory is not stored in muscles or fascia, and "repressed memory recovered
through the body" has a long and damaging history. Evidence-based trauma treatments exist
and are named.
**Source leads, to verify.** Van der Kolk (2014), *The Body Keeps the Score*. McNally
(2003), *Remembering Trauma*. Otgaar et al. (2019), the return of the repressed. The
current clinical guidelines for post-traumatic stress disorder (NICE or the ISTSS
guidelines), for what treatment the evidence supports.
**Drawing.** The figure with what is documented drawn on the body (sleep, startle, heart),
and nothing drawn in the muscles.
**Application.** The patient who has been told her back pain is her childhood, sorted by
Safety: does anything need action, and with whom.

## Finale

### 10 · the-book · open

**Claim, the book's own.** The body speaks first. The mind explains second. Threat
detection and prediction quietly shape what feels true.
**Hook, draft.** For nine episodes I have held other people's claims to one standard. This
is the episode where I hold my own book to it.
**Kernel.** Interoception and predictive accounts of emotion are a live research
programme with real findings and real disputes. Where the book's phrasing goes beyond
them is said plainly. Then the season's hidden structure is shown: three acts, three
questions, Time, Attention, Safety, the sorting tool that was behind every application
segment.
**Source leads, to verify.** Craig (2002), interoception. Barrett (2017), *How Emotions
Are Made*. Seth (2021), *Being You*. Khalsa et al. (2018), the interoception roadmap.
Candia-Rivera et al. (2022), already in `CITATIONS.md`, for where attention to the body
is and is not shown to change it.
**Drawing.** The figure one last time, with the three questions placed on it: Time at the
clock in the head, Attention at the eyes, Safety at the chest.
**Application.** The season's nine composites, revisited in one minute each with the
question that sorted them.
