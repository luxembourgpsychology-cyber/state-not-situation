## 1. Strogatz (2015), Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos

Strogatz, S. H. (2015). *Nonlinear dynamics and chaos: With applications to physics, biology, chemistry, and engineering* (2nd ed.). Westview Press. ISBN 978-0-8133-4910-7.

The book exists as cited. The second edition is dated 2015 (copyright page: "First published 2015 by Westview Press"; some retailers list 2014). A third edition appeared in 2024, so the series should name the second explicitly. Chapter 3 is "Bifurcations", chapter 8 is "Bifurcations Revisited" with section 8.1 "Saddle-Node, Transcritical, and Pitchfork Bifurcations" and section 8.2 "Hopf Bifurcations"; chapter 10 is "One-Dimensional Maps", where period doubling is treated.

The claim must be narrowed. Strogatz's catalogue of local bifurcations of fixed points is saddle-node, transcritical and pitchfork for one-dimensional flows, with Hopf added in two dimensions. Period doubling is a bifurcation of periodic orbits and of maps, not of fixed points of a flow, and belongs to the chaos chapters. He also treats global bifurcations (saddle-node of cycles, infinite-period, homoclinic) in section 8.4, which are not local at all. The honest sentence is: "the catalogue of ways a fixed point can lose stability is short: saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork, Hopf." Drop period doubling from that list, or move it to a sentence about cycles.

## 2. Izhikevich (2007), Dynamical Systems in Neuroscience

Izhikevich, E. M. (2007). *Dynamical systems in neuroscience: The geometry of excitability and bursting*. MIT Press. ISBN 978-0-262-09043-8.

The book exists as cited (MIT Press Computational Neuroscience series, 2007; paperback 2010, ISBN 978-0-262-51420-0). Its chapter on bifurcations covers saddle-node (fold), saddle-node on invariant circle, and supercritical and subcritical Andronov-Hopf, and it maps Hodgkin's Class 1 and Class 2 excitability onto them.

The claim is right in outline but must be narrowed in two ways. First, Izhikevich's central result is that there are four, not two, codimension-one bifurcations by which a resting neuron can start to fire as injected current rises: saddle-node, saddle-node on invariant circle, subcritical Andronov-Hopf and supercritical Andronov-Hopf. The saddle-node pair gives integrators (Class 1, firing can start at arbitrarily low frequency); the Hopf pair gives resonators (Class 2, firing starts at a nonzero frequency, with subthreshold oscillations). Second, the sort is by dynamical regime, not by anatomical cell type: the same neuron can move from one class to another under neuromodulation or a change in conductances. Say "depending on the neuron's dynamical regime, which Izhikevich sorts into integrators and resonators", not "depending on the cell type".

## 3. Scheffer et al. (2009), Nature

Scheffer, M., Bascompte, J., Brock, W. A., Brovkin, V., Carpenter, S. R., Dakos, V., Held, H., van Nes, E. H., Rietkerk, M., & Sugihara, G. (2009). Early-warning signals for critical transitions. *Nature, 461*(7260), 53–59. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08227

Confirmed. The paper is a review arguing that systems approaching a fold bifurcation show generic signatures: critical slowing down, which appears as rising autocorrelation and variance and slower recovery from small perturbations, plus skewness and flickering. Its evidence in 2009 was mostly models and retrospective records (paleoclimate, some ecological time series); the shallow lake is its stock example of a system with alternative stable states and hysteresis.

Both attached claims survive, but each should be pinned to the paper that measured it rather than to the review alone. The clear-to-turbid lake with hysteresis is set out in Scheffer, M., Carpenter, S., Foley, J. A., Folke, C., & Walker, B. (2001). Catastrophic shifts in ecosystems. *Nature, 413*(6856), 591–596. https://doi.org/10.1038/35098000, which explains the folded curve, two stable states and why restoring nutrient load to its pre-collapse value is not enough to clear the water. That critical slowing down is measurable before a real transition was shown after the review: in a whole lake by Carpenter, S. R., Cole, J. J., Pace, M. L., Batt, R., Brock, W. A., Cline, T., Coloso, J., Hodgson, J. R., Kitchell, J. F., Seekell, D. A., Smith, L., & Weidel, B. (2011). Early warnings of regime shifts: A whole-ecosystem experiment. *Science, 332*(6033), 1079–1082. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1203672, where rising variance and slowed recovery appeared more than a year before the food web completed its shift; and in a living laboratory system by Veraart, A. J., Faassen, E. J., Dakos, V., van Nes, E. H., Lürling, M., & Scheffer, M. (2012). Recovery rates reflect distance to a tipping point in a living system. *Nature, 481*(7381), 357–359. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10723 (cyanobacteria; a corrigendum at *Nature, 484*, 404 corrected a scaling in Figure 1 and the description of the perturbation). Two caveats belong on camera: the 2009 review itself says such shifts "typically occur unannounced", and later reviews report a growing list of transitions with no slowing down beforehand. Say "can precede" rather than "precedes".

## 4. Haken, Kelso and Bunz (1985), Biological Cybernetics

Haken, H., Kelso, J. A. S., & Bunz, H. (1985). A theoretical model of phase transitions in human hand movements. *Biological Cybernetics, 51*(5), 347–356. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00336922

Confirmed. The paper is the model: a potential for the relative phase of two rhythmically moving fingers or hands with minima at in-phase and anti-phase; as the ratio of its two coefficients falls with rising frequency, the anti-phase minimum flattens and disappears and the system jumps to in-phase. In the reduced relative-phase equation the anti-phase state loses stability through a pitchfork (when the two hands are symmetric; with asymmetry it becomes a saddle-node), so "pitchfork-type" is fair for the model as published.

The claim survives but the measurement must be credited to the right paper. HKB 1985 reproduces an experiment, it does not report one. The human data are Kelso, J. A. S. (1984). Phase transitions and critical behavior in human bimanual coordination. *American Journal of Physiology: Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology, 246*(6), R1000–R1004. The two predictions that make it a bifurcation rather than a switch were tested afterwards: critical fluctuations in Kelso, J. A. S., Scholz, J. P., & Schöner, G. (1986). Nonequilibrium phase transitions in coordinated biological motion: Critical fluctuations. *Physics Letters A, 118*(6), 279–284, and critical slowing down in Scholz, J. P., Kelso, J. A. S., & Schöner, G. (1987). Nonequilibrium phase transitions in coordinated biological motion: Critical slowing down and switching time. *Physics Letters A, 123*(8), 390–394. https://doi.org/10.1016/0375-9601(87)90038-7. For episode 3 the 1987 paper is the one that matters: relaxation time of the anti-phase pattern lengthens as the critical frequency nears, in people, with a metronome.

## 5. Zahler and Sussmann (1977), Nature

Zahler, R. S., & Sussmann, H. J. (1977). Claims and accomplishments of applied catastrophe theory. *Nature, 269*(5631), 759–763. https://doi.org/10.1038/269759a0

Confirmed. The authors examined several representative applications of catastrophe theory to biology and the social sciences (the Zeeman-school models of the period) and found them, in the abstract's words, "characterised by incorrect reasoning, far-fetched assumptions, erroneous consequences, and exaggerated claims", concluding that the theory had made no significant contribution to those fields. Nature printed replies in December 1977 (vol. 270, 381–384) and the authors answered in February 1978 (vol. 271, 401), restricting their verdict to the biological and social applications and leaving physics and engineering aside.

The claim must be narrowed. The paper does not survey "most" 1970s applications and does not use the phrase "untestable pattern-matching"; it dissects a handful of representative models. The defensible sentence is: "In 1977 two mathematicians went through the best-known social and biological applications of catastrophe theory and found diagrams drawn over data nobody had measured, assumptions nobody had tested, and claims the mathematics could not carry." The brief's own wording, "a diagram drawn over data nobody measured", is a fair paraphrase of their complaint about fitting a cusp to unquantified variables. Attribute "untestable" to the series' judgement, not to the paper.

## 6. Dickerson and Kemeny (2004), Psychological Bulletin

Dickerson, S. S., & Kemeny, M. E. (2004). Acute stressors and cortisol responses: A theoretical integration and synthesis of laboratory research. *Psychological Bulletin, 130*(3), 355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355

Confirmed as a meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies. Its headline is that tasks combining uncontrollability with social-evaluative threat produce the largest cortisol and ACTH responses and the longest recovery. On timing, the paper states that "the peak cortisol response occurs 21–40 min from onset of acute psychological stressors", with a significant quadratic fit of effect size against time that holds in a reduced sample. Excerpts also show that when samples are timed from the end of the stressor, the 0 to 20 minute window carries a larger effect (d = 0.38) than 21 to 40 (d = 0.26); the two statements are consistent, since a stressor of ten to twenty minutes puts both windows in the same place on the clock, but the series should pick one reference point and say which.

The claim fails as stated. "Roughly 15 to 20 minutes" is too early. Write: "salivary cortisol peaks roughly twenty to forty minutes after a stressor begins, and takes longer still to come down", and cite the paper for that window. For the series' second clock this is the right number: autonomic in seconds, cortisol in tens of minutes.

## 7. Lightman and Conway-Campbell (2010), Nature Reviews Neuroscience

Lightman, S. L., & Conway-Campbell, B. L. (2010). The crucial role of pulsatile activity of the HPA axis for continuous dynamic equilibration. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 11*(10), 710–718. https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2914

Confirmed (PMID 20842176). The review argues that glucocorticoid secretion is not a feedback-held steady state but an oscillation: an hourly ultradian pulse superimposed on the circadian rhythm, and that this pulsatility is itself needed for tissues to respond normally, because glucocorticoid receptor binding and transcription track each pulse. The authors name the resulting state "continuous dynamic equilibration".

The claim survives. One hedge to carry: the hourly figure is best characterised in the rat; human cortisol is also pulsatile, with pulses in the same general range, and the review treats both. Say "roughly hourly pulses, riding on the day's rhythm", which is what the abstract says, and do not quote a tighter human period from this source. For the series this paper is also the plainest statement that "a biomarker is a trajectory, not a level": its argument is that the pattern of cortisol carries information that a single level does not.

## 8. Jamieson, Nock and Mendes (2012), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General

Jamieson, J. P., Nock, M. K., & Mendes, W. B. (2012). Mind over matter: Reappraising arousal improves cardiovascular and cognitive responses to stress. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 141*(3), 417–422. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025719

Confirmed (PMID 21942377; published online September 2011). A brief report. Participants did a Trier Social Stress Test after being told either that stress arousal is functional and aids performance (reappraisal), to ignore the source of stress (attention control), or nothing. Cardiac output and total peripheral resistance were measured by impedance cardiography; reactivity was the first minute of the speech minus the last minute of baseline. The reappraisal group showed higher cardiac output and lower total peripheral resistance than controls, the "challenge" profile, and reduced attentional bias to threat. One excerpt gives the cardiac output contrast as F(1, 47) = 6.86, so the sample is about fifty.

The claim survives as stated: appraisal changed a measured vascular response, in the direction predicted, on the same clock as the stressor. Two things to say on camera. The sample is small and the result is from one laboratory; the finding has since been built on by the same group rather than widely replicated by others, and the series should not present it as settled. And it is evidence about appraisal, not about attention to the signal: the attention-control group, told to ignore the stressor, did not show the effect. For the brief's third claim this paper carries "direction set by appraisal"; it does not carry "attending to a bodily signal changes it".

## 9. Lehrer and Gevirtz (2014), Frontiers in Psychology

Lehrer, P. M., & Gevirtz, R. (2014). Heart rate variability biofeedback: How and why does it work? *Frontiers in Psychology, 5*, Article 756. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756

Confirmed. This is a Hypothesis and Theory article, not a study. It proposes that breathing at the cardiovascular system's resonance frequency (around 0.1 Hz, roughly six breaths a minute, lower for taller people) produces very large heart rate oscillations because respiratory sinus arrhythmia and the baroreflex loop fall into phase, and that repeated training strengthens baroreflex gain. It reviews the evidence rather than adding to it.

The claim must be narrowed in its sourcing, not its substance. That slow breathing near resonance frequency produces high-amplitude heart rate oscillation at once, within the first minutes of paced breathing, is a measurement, and it belongs to Vaschillo, E., Lehrer, P., Rishe, N., & Konstantinov, M. (2002). Heart rate variability biofeedback as a method for assessing baroreflex function: A preliminary study of resonance in the cardiovascular system. *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback, 27*(1), 1–27. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1014587304314, and Vaschillo, E. G., Vaschillo, B., & Lehrer, P. M. (2006). Characteristics of resonance in heart rate variability stimulated by biofeedback. *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback, 31*(2), 129–142. Cite Lehrer and Gevirtz for the mechanism and Vaschillo for the measurement. Also say "increases the amplitude of heart rate oscillation" rather than "increases heart rate variability" without qualification: the immediate effect is on the breathing-frequency band of HRV, and the durable changes in vagal tone and baroreflex gain that the review discusses come from weeks of training (Lehrer et al., 2003, *Psychosomatic Medicine*, is the usual source for the latter and was not checked here).

## 10. van de Leemput et al. (2014), PNAS

van de Leemput, I. A., Wichers, M., Cramer, A. O. J., Borsboom, D., Tuerlinckx, F., Kuppens, P., van Nes, E. H., Viechtbauer, W., Giltay, E. J., Aggen, S. H., Derom, C., Jacobs, N., Kendler, K. S., van der Maas, H. L. J., Neale, M. C., Peeters, F., Thiery, E., Zachar, P., & Scheffer, M. (2014). Critical slowing down as early warning for the onset and termination of depression. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 111*(1), 87–92. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1312114110

Confirmed. Using experience-sampling data from two cohorts, the authors report that people who later shifted into or out of depression had higher temporal autocorrelation, variance and cross-correlation among momentary emotions, which they read as critical slowing down in mood.

The claim survives: it is the first and most-cited attempt, and its status is contested. The contest is on record. Bos, E. H., & de Jonge, P. (2014). "Critical slowing down in depression" is a great idea that still needs empirical proof. *PNAS, 111*(10), E878. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1323672111, argue that the indicators were computed between people, not within a person over time, and could reflect higher baseline emotion rather than slowing; the authors replied in Wichers, M., et al. (2014). Reply to Bos and De Jonge: Between-subject data do provide first empirical support for critical slowing down in depression. *PNAS, 111*(10), E879. Within-person tests since have been mixed. Schreuder, M. J., Hartman, C. A., George, S. V., Menne-Lothmann, C., Decoster, J., van Winkel, R., Delespaul, P., De Hert, M., Derom, C., Thiery, E., Rutten, B. P. F., Jacobs, N., van Os, J., Wigman, J. T. W., & Wichers, M. (2020). Early warning signals in psychopathology: What do they tell? *BMC Medicine, 18*, Article 269, found signals for one symptom domain and not for depression. Helmich, M. A., Smit, A. C., Bringmann, L. F., Schreuder, M. J., Oldehinkel, A. J., Wichers, M., & Snippe, E. (2023). Detecting impending symptom transitions using early-warning signals in individuals receiving treatment for depression. *Clinical Psychological Science, 11*(6), 994–1010. https://doi.org/10.1177/21677026221137006, found autocorrelation rising in both people who transitioned and people who did not. Tonge, N. A., Miller, J. P., Kharasch, E. D., Lenze, E. J., & Rodebaugh, T. L. (2024). An investigation of the potential clinical utility of critical slowing down as an early warning sign for recurrence of depression. *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry, 82*, Article 101922, found a signal in the one participant who relapsed and false positives in those who did not. On the theory side, Dablander, F., Pichler, A., Cika, A., & Bacilieri, A. (2023). Anticipating critical transitions in psychological systems using early warning signals: Theoretical and practical considerations. *Psychological Methods, 28*(4), 765–790. https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000450, show by simulation that whether the signals appear depends on the system's specifics and is fragile to noise. The field's own summary is Helmich, M. A., Schreuder, M. J., Bringmann, L. F., Riese, H., Snippe, E., & Smit, A. C. (2024). Slow down and be critical before using early warning signals in psychopathology. *Nature Reviews Psychology, 3*(11), 767–780. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-024-00369-y, which concludes that the signals have little empirical support when applied to one person's emotion time series. The series can state all of this; it is exactly the negative case episode 3 needs. One wording change: "closest attempt" should become "first and best-known attempt", since later single-person studies came closer to the actual test and still did not settle it.

## The interoception question

Asked for: a primary source showing that attending to one's own heartbeat changes heart rate or its variability, not only self-report.

For heart rate variability, none confirmed. I found no study in which directing attention to the heart, with breathing controlled, produced a measured change in HRV. Studies that touch the question manipulate something else (fasting, physical activity, biofeedback over weeks) or treat HRV as a confound of task accuracy rather than an outcome.

For heart rate itself there is one recent primary source. Candia-Rivera, D., Sappia, M. S., Horschig, J. M., Colier, W. N. J. M., & Valenza, G. (2022). Confounding effects of heart rate, breathing rate, and frontal fNIRS on interoception. *Scientific Reports, 12*, Article 20701. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-25119-z. During a heartbeat counting task, heart rate and breathing rate fell relative to a control condition and cardiorespiratory coupling changed; the authors offer the fall as part of the physiological basis of attending to the heart, and they note that earlier studies had reported non-significant heart rate changes during the same task. They also state that heart rate cannot be controlled voluntarily through such tasks the way it can through neurofeedback. The confound they name is the one the series would have to answer on camera: attending to the heart slows the breath, and a slower breath moves heart rate and HRV on its own, so the effect cannot yet be assigned to attention rather than respiration.

This is a finding, and it bears on the brief's third claim. "Attending to a bodily signal changes that signal's trajectory" is supported for appraisal (Jamieson) and for breathing (Vaschillo), where the act of attending is also an act of control. For the heartbeat, which cannot be controlled directly, the evidence is one 2022 study of heart rate against a background of null results, and nothing for variability. The claim as the brief states it should be marked proposed, not demonstrated, or rewritten around breath and appraisal, where it is demonstrated. What to look up next: heartbeat-evoked potential studies that report heart rate and respiration as control variables across attention conditions (Petzschner et al., 2019, *NeuroImage*, "Focus of attention modulates the heartbeat evoked potential", tested for cardiac differences between conditions; I could not read the result), and any within-subject design that paces breathing while switching attention between heart and an external rhythm.
