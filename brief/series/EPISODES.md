## Episode 0. A state is not a feeling and not a situation

**The one idea.** A state is the pattern the body is holding, and a pattern has its own
stability, which neither the feeling that reports it nor the situation that provoked it has.

**The demonstrated case.** Two people sit at a table and tap their index fingers, one on
each hand, in time with a metronome. They start anti-phase: one finger down while the other
is up. The metronome speeds up a little at a time. For a while nothing changes except the
tempo. Then, at a rate that differs a little from person to person, the fingers switch into
moving together, in-phase, without being asked to and without the person deciding to. Just
before the switch, the anti-phase pattern wobbles: the timing between the two fingers
scatters more than it did [citation needed: the fluctuation measurements of Kelso, Scholz
and Schöner, 1986, not yet verified]. After the switch the wobble settles. Slow the
metronome back down and the fingers stay in-phase; they do not return to anti-phase at the
rate where they left it. The thing that changed was not the finger, not the muscle and not
the instruction. It was the stability of a pattern between two fingers, and the measurable
quantity was the relative phase between them. The switch was measured in people by Kelso
(1984) and given its equation, a pitchfork-type loss of stability, by Haken, Kelso, and
Bunz (1985).

**The ordinary moment.** You are at your desk on a Tuesday and someone asks whether you are
all right. You say yes, because the situation is fine and the feeling you can name is mild.
But your jaw has been set since the morning meeting, your shoulders have not come down, and
you have read the same paragraph three times. Nothing in the room explains it. Nothing you
would call an emotion covers it. Something is being held, and it was being held before you
noticed.

**The negative case.** A reflex looks like a state and is not. The hand that leaves a hot
pan is a change in the body with no deciding involved, but it has no stability of its own:
remove the heat and the hand is simply a hand again, at once. The one feature that tells
them apart is what happens when the cause is withdrawn. A reflex returns with the cause. A
state has to find its own way back, and it does not always take the same road.

**Where the strict version ends.** "Relative phase between two fingers has been measured
and its stability has been shown to change. That a body has a quantity like that for what
you call being fine is my proposal, not a finding."

**Closing question.** What is your body holding right now that no feeling word covers and
no situation explains?

**Optional demonstration.** Honest and cheap: a metronome app, a table, and the host's own
two index fingers, filmed from above, from anti-phase at a slow tempo through the switch.
It will happen on camera or it will not, and either is worth showing.

## Episode 1. Sudden change from gradual cause

**The one idea.** A body can tip all at once under a cause that never jumped, because what
runs out is not the cause's patience but the stability of the state it was in.

**The demonstrated case.** A single neuron sits at rest while a small, steady current is
passed into it. The current is turned up slowly. The cell's membrane voltage rises slightly
and settles, rises slightly and settles. Nothing fires. Then, with no step in the current,
the cell begins to produce spikes, and in one common class of neuron the first spikes come
at an arbitrarily slow rhythm that quickens as the current climbs further. What has
happened is geometric. At low current the cell has a resting point and, near it, a
threshold; the two sit a little apart, and small disturbances fall back to rest. As current
rises the two draw together. At the critical current they meet and cancel, and the resting
point simply no longer exists. The cell is not pushed into firing; it has nowhere left to
rest (Izhikevich, 2007). The same collision of a stable and an unstable point under one
slowly turned parameter is the saddle-node bifurcation, and it is the first transition
shape in the catalogue, not because neurons are special but because stability allows few
ways to disappear (Strogatz, 2015).

**The ordinary moment.** The week has been long but ordinary. You have been managing. On
Thursday evening you drop a jar lid, it rolls under the fridge, and you find yourself
sitting on the kitchen floor unable to stand up for a minute. The jar lid did nothing. The
week did nothing on any single day. You were fine, and then you were not, and the honest
account is that there was no event in between.

**The negative case.** A door slamming looks like this and is not. You jump, your heart
thumps, you were calm and now you are not. But that change had a cause of its own size,
arriving all at once. The one observable feature is whether the input moved before the
output did. In the neuron, the current climbed smoothly and the voltage jumped. After a
slammed door, the input jumped and the body followed it. Sudden change from sudden cause is
ordinary. Sudden change from gradual cause is the idea.

**Where the strict version ends.** "The neuron's current was measured as it rose. Nobody
has measured the slow cause in your Thursday. I am proposing that one was there."

**Closing question.** The last time you tipped, what had been climbing for days that
nobody, including you, was reading?

**Optional demonstration.** None that is honest. A spilling glass tips at a threshold but
its mechanism is a different one, and showing it would teach the wrong picture.

## Episode 2. Hysteresis: the way back is not the way there

**The one idea.** A body that tipped at one level of cause does not tip back at that
level, because the new state has learned to hold itself.

**The demonstrated case.** A shallow lake is clear. Nutrients run into it from farms year
on year, and for a long time the water stays clear while the load rises. Then, over a
season or two, it turns turbid, green with algae. The farms are regulated, the load is
brought back down to what it was when the lake clouded, and the lake does not clear. It
has to be brought a good deal lower before it does. The reason is in the lake itself. In
the clear state, rooted plants on the bottom hold the sediment and take up nutrients,
which keeps the water clear enough for them to grow. Turbid water shades them out, the
sediment is freed, and the algae now have the nutrients the plants had been holding. Each
state maintains the conditions for itself. Between the two levels of load, both states are
stable, and the lake stays in whichever it is in. The clouding and the clearing happen at
different places on the same axis, and that gap is hysteresis (Scheffer et al., 2009). The
shape is again general: a fold with two stable branches, which Strogatz (2015) draws for a
system with nothing to do with water.

**The ordinary moment.** Something was said at lunch, and your body went up. It took one
sentence. Afterwards your partner says the sentence was not meant as it sounded, and you
believe them. Your body does not come down. They say it again, differently, and it still
does not. By the evening it has, but it took four reassurances to undo what one remark did.
Nothing was unreasonable in you. The level that brings you down is simply not the level
that sent you up.

**The negative case.** Slow recovery looks like hysteresis and is not. After a hard
twenty minutes, cortisol in the blood keeps rising after the task has ended and is highest
around twenty to forty minutes after it began (Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004). That looks like a
body refusing to come back. But it is a clock: given time and nothing more, it returns.
The one observable feature is whether the return depends on time or on level. If reversing
the cause to where the change happened and waiting is enough, that is lag. If waiting is
not enough and the cause must be taken further back than it ever came forward, that is
hysteresis.

**Where the strict version ends.** "The lake's two thresholds were measured. The gap
between what sends a person up and what brings them down is something I propose has the
same shape. Nobody has measured it."

**Closing question.** Where in your week does it take more to come back than it took to go?

**Optional demonstration.** None honest at the table.

## Episode 3. Critical slowing down: the week before you tip

**The one idea.** A system close to tipping shows it first by taking longer to recover
from small knocks, before anything else has visibly changed.

**The demonstrated case.** Consider the same lake, still clear, with its load creeping up
toward the fold. A windy day stirs the water and it clouds briefly, then clears. Years
earlier it would have cleared in days. Now it takes weeks. Nothing has tipped; the state is
the same state. But the force pulling the lake back to clear is weakening as the fold
approaches, because at the fold that force reaches zero. Two things follow in the record.
The lake's condition on one day predicts its condition on the next more closely than it
used to, since disturbances linger. And its condition wanders more widely, since nothing
pulls it back as hard. Rising recovery time, rising autocorrelation and rising variance,
observed together while the state has not changed, are the early-warning signature of a
critical transition, and they appear in models and in records of lakes, climate and
other systems before the fold is reached (Scheffer et al., 2009). The mechanism is the
general one: near a saddle-node, the rate of return to equilibrium goes to zero (Strogatz,
2015).

**The ordinary moment.** It is the week before. Nothing has happened yet. A colleague's
short reply sits in you until lunch, when last month it would have been gone in a minute.
A bad night's sleep takes two days to clear instead of one. You notice you are swinging
more: high at ten, flat at eleven, with no reason you can point to. You are still fine.
The word is accurate. It is just taking longer each time to get back to it.

**The negative case.** Tiredness looks like this and is not. When you are run down,
everything is slower to recover, and the slowness tracks the sleep you have lost; it
lifts when you sleep. The one observable feature is the wandering. A tired system is slow
and flat. A system near a threshold is slow and noisy, with recovery time and variance
rising together while the apparent load has not moved. [citation needed: a study of
day-to-day affective variance and recovery time in people before a measured transition,
to say whether this feature has been observed in a human record at all.]

**Where the strict version ends.** "Slowing down before a fold has been shown in lakes
and in models. Whether a person's week carries the same signature before a tip is my
proposal. I know of no measurement of it."

**Closing question.** What took longer to shake off this week than it did a month ago?

**Optional demonstration.** Honest and cheap, with one caveat said aloud. A fingertip
heart-rate reading on screen. The host breathes slowly, at about six breaths a minute, and
the amplitude of the heart rate's swing with the breath rises within the first minutes,
because the baroreflex loop that couples blood pressure and heart rate has a delay of a few
seconds and resonates near that rate (mechanism: Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; measurement:
Vaschillo, Lehrer, Rishe, & Konstantinov, 2002). Then the host attends to a sound in the room
instead, with no instruction about breathing, and the swing changes. The caveat: this does
not demonstrate critical slowing down. It demonstrates the brief's third claim in the
form it is actually shown: where attending to a signal is also controlling it, as with the
breath, the signal's trajectory changes. Whether attending to the heartbeat alone, which
cannot be controlled, changes the heartbeat is proposed and not shown. It belongs here
because it shows a recovery time being watched by someone who is part of the system being
watched.
