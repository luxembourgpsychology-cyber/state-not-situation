## Press release

PRESS RELEASE

For immediate release

Luxembourg, 16 October 2026

State. Not Situation., the first book by Luxembourg clinical psychologist Ivana Budišin, published 15 October 2026

Why your body decides what a moment means before you do

Budisin Publishing, Luxembourg, has published State. Not Situation., the first book by Ivana Budišin, a clinical psychologist living and working in Luxembourg. The paperback and the Kindle edition went on sale on 15 October 2026.

The back cover puts the book's question in two sentences: "You may not be reacting to the world. You may be reacting to your state."

It continues: "We often mistake a state for a fact. A person seems hostile. A task feels impossible. A relationship appears wrong. A whole day looks lost. But what feels true is not produced by the situation alone. Sleep, hunger, stress, timing, attention, memory and threat detection affect what we notice and how we interpret it. Together, they shape the explanation that feels most convincing. State. Not Situation. examines the gap between what happened and what it felt like it meant."

The book opens, on pages 1 to 3, with the flight of John F. Kennedy Jr. on the evening of 16 July 1999, and with what his instruments showed and his body told him. Page 3 draws the line the rest of the book follows: "You operate a body that produces signals and your mind often treats those signals as truth." And: "But the body's confident weather report about the world is only a draft."

From page 7: "The chapters that follow are organised around three systems that shape how your body's signals get weighted before your mind builds a story." Page 9 gives the three questions, under Time, Attention and Safety. Time: "What came before this moment? Sleep, meals, caffeine, recovery, the hour." Attention: "What keeps pulling me back? A message, a thought, a cue, an unfinished task." Safety: "What seems at stake? What have I observed, what am I assuming, and does anything need action?" Page 7 says of them: "They are not brain regions or neural pathways. They are a sorting tool, a way to ask three questions when everything feels wrong at once."

Each chapter carries an evidence marker. In the author's words: High, "Replicated or robust evidence." Medium, "Suggestive evidence with meaningful uncertainty." Low, "Plausible hypothesis or emerging evidence." The closing section, The Scientific Heartbeat, lists for each chapter "the work the chapter is built on, and a list of the work that limits it, complicates it, or explains the same evidence differently", because, as page 242 puts it, "Leaving them out would make the argument look tidier than it is."

[DRAFT QUOTE FOR IVANA TO APPROVE OR REWRITE] Ivana Budišin said: "It is less interested in teaching you to trust your instincts than in showing you what, exactly, you are trusting. [DRAFT, NEW SENTENCE FOR IVANA TO ACCEPT OR STRIKE: It offers questions, not a diagnosis.]"

The author describes the book as being "for anyone who has ever been certain about what a situation meant, then discovered that something else was happening. And for readers interested in the psychology of how we notice, interpret and revise the world around us."

The foreword is by Dr Kristina Herber, Managing Director, AIHE Academic Institute for Higher Education, who writes: "Our first interpretation is not necessarily wrong. But it does not have to be our last."
